## SERIES
**Title:** Sherlock Holmes
**Author:** Arthur Conan Doyle
**Logline:** A brilliant, emotionally armored consulting detective and the steady doctor who chronicles him take on the impossible crimes of Victorian London — and the one adversary clever enough to nearly end them both.
**Note:** The canon spans four novels and dozens of short stories; this demo focuses on five foundational books that carry Holmes and Watson from their first meeting through the Moriarty conspiracy and its aftermath. Told almost entirely through Watson's eyes, it is as much a story about friendship and observation as it is about crime.

---

## BOOK
**Title:** A Study in Scarlet
**Order:** 1
**Status:** published
**Worlds:** Victorian London, 221B Baker Street
**Blurb:** A discharged army doctor named John Watson, searching for cheap lodgings, is introduced to an eccentric stranger who can deduce a man's entire history from a glance. Their first case together — a murder with the word RACHE scrawled in blood — reveals just how far Sherlock Holmes's methods can reach, and how far one man's grievance can travel across an ocean and half a lifetime to collect its due.

## BOOK
**Title:** The Sign of Four
**Order:** 2
**Status:** published
**Worlds:** Victorian London, 221B Baker Street
**Blurb:** A young woman receives a mysterious pearl every year from an anonymous benefactor, then an invitation to recover a vanished treasure. Holmes and Watson are pulled into a story of stolen Agra treasure, a wooden-legged killer, and a debt of blood from the Indian Mutiny — while Watson finds himself falling for the client he's meant to be protecting.

## BOOK
**Title:** The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes
**Order:** 3
**Status:** published
**Worlds:** Victorian London, 221B Baker Street
**Blurb:** Twelve short cases, newly married Watson's first collection of Holmes's exploits for the public. Includes "A Scandal in Bohemia," in which Holmes meets the only person ever to outwit him — and loses, gracefully, for the first time.

## BOOK
**Title:** The Memoirs of Sherlock Holmes
**Order:** 4
**Status:** published
**Worlds:** Victorian London, 221B Baker Street, Reichenbach Falls
**Blurb:** A darker, more introspective set of cases, culminating in "The Final Problem" — Watson's account of Holmes's collision with Professor Moriarty, the "Napoleon of crime," and their shared plunge over the Reichenbach Falls. The book that was meant to end the series.

## BOOK
**Title:** The Hound of the Baskervilles
**Order:** 5
**Status:** published
**Worlds:** Victorian London, Dartmoor / Baskerville Hall, 221B Baker Street
**Blurb:** A family curse, a spectral hound, and a death on the moor bring Holmes back from apparent retirement (in the story's internal chronology, a case predating Reichenbach) to protect the last heir of Baskerville Hall from a beast that may not be entirely supernatural — or entirely animal.

## CHARACTER
**Name:** Sherlock Holmes
**Role:** Consulting detective
**One-line:** The world's first and only consulting detective, a reasoning machine who treats emotion as a contaminant in an otherwise perfect instrument.
**Aliases:** Sigerson (used during his years in hiding after Reichenbach)
**Relationships:**
  - **To:** John Watson — **Type:** friend/chronicler — **Note:** His only real intimacy; Watson is both the one person who can reach him and the one person who documents him to the world.
  - **To:** Mycroft Holmes — **Type:** brother — **Note:** The one person smarter than Holmes, though too indolent to ever act on it.
  - **To:** Professor Moriarty — **Type:** nemesis — **Note:** The only adversary who ever matched him — an equal and opposite intellect, which is precisely why Holmes cannot let the matter rest.
  - **To:** Mrs. Hudson — **Type:** landlady — **Note:** Long-suffering keeper of 221B, tolerates the chemistry experiments, the revolver practice, and the visitors at all hours.
  - **To:** Inspector Lestrade — **Type:** professional rival/ally — **Note:** Scotland Yard's most persistent visitor to Baker Street; exasperated by Holmes, but always comes back for help.
  - **To:** Irene Adler — **Type:** the one who bested him — **Note:** The only person, in Watson's telling, ever to outwit Holmes — and a woman, which unsettles him more than the defeat itself.
**Linked to (worlds/threads):**
  - **World:** 221B Baker Street — **Note:** His rooms, his laboratory, his stage.
  - **Thread:** The Moriarty Conspiracy — **Note:** The throughline that defines the back half of his career as Watson records it.
**Arcs (one per book where the character grows):**
  - **Book:** A Study in Scarlet — **Status:** closed —
    **Want:** To prove his deductive method against a real, unsolved case — to be taken seriously by the official police — **Need:** To find a partner who can witness and translate his gift for an audience that doesn't understand him — **Wound:** Years of being dismissed as an eccentric obsessive by everyone except his own intellect —
    **Scores:** proactivity 9, relatability 3, capability 9 —
    **Story-circle steps:** you, need, go, search, find, take, return —
    **Why these scores:** He drives every event in the investigation and is already formidably skilled, but Watson (and the reader) still see him as a curiosity rather than a person — relatability is the last thing to arrive.
  - **Book:** The Sign of Four — **Status:** closed —
    **Want:** A case difficult enough to hold off the boredom that drives him to cocaine — **Need:** To reckon with the fact that his methods have a human cost, not just an intellectual payoff — **Wound:** A mind so fast that ordinary life moves too slowly to bear —
    **Scores:** proactivity 9, relatability 4, capability 9 —
    **Story-circle steps:** need, go, search, find, return —
    **Why these scores:** Slight relatability bump — watching Watson fall for Mary Morstan forces Holmes to notice, briefly, what he's opted out of.
  - **Book:** The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes — **Status:** closed —
    **Want:** To keep winning — to remain the undefeated reasoning machine — **Need:** To be shown, once, that intellect isn't the only kind of power in the room — **Wound:** A pride so total it has never had to survive a real loss —
    **Scores:** proactivity 8, relatability 6, capability 10 —
    **Story-circle steps:** you, need, go, search, find, take —
    **Why these scores:** Capability peaks here — this is Holmes at his most dazzling across a dozen cases — but "A Scandal in Bohemia" cracks him open just enough that relatability jumps; losing to Irene Adler is the first time he reads as fully human.
  - **Book:** The Memoirs of Sherlock Holmes — **Status:** open —
    **Want:** To end Moriarty for good, whatever it costs — **Need:** To accept that some victories require disappearing — that being right isn't the same as being safe — **Wound:** The horror of discovering his equal exists, and that matching wits with him means matching risks —
    **Scores:** proactivity 10, relatability 7, capability 10 —
    **Story-circle steps:** need, go, search, find, take, return, change —
    **Why these scores:** Everything peaks. This is the most proactive and most human Holmes gets in the demo — he chooses Watson's safety over his own pride, which is new for him, and then apparently dies for it.
  - **Book:** The Hound of the Baskervilles — **Status:** closed —
    **Want:** To debunk the supernatural explanation and expose the human hand behind it — **Need:** To trust Watson enough to send him ahead alone and stay hidden himself — a real test of the partnership under strain — **Wound:** Chronologically set before Reichenbach in the story's internal timeline, but read after it — the shadow of his "death" colors how the audience sees every risk he takes here —
    **Scores:** proactivity 9, relatability 6, capability 9 —
    **Story-circle steps:** you, need, go, search, find, take, return —
    **Why these scores:** A slight step back from Memoirs' peak — this case is more procedural, but the trust he places in Watson (going undercover on the moor alone) is its own quiet growth.
**Lessons (optional, moral/emotional beats):**
  - **Book:** The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes — **Trigger:** Irene Adler outmaneuvers him and leaves before he can act — **Lesson:** Intellect alone does not guarantee victory; underestimating an opponent's resourcefulness is itself a failure of observation — **Becomes:** Slightly less contemptuous of "the softer passions" as a category of intelligence — **Status:** partial
  - **Book:** The Memoirs of Sherlock Holmes — **Trigger:** Realizing Moriarty threatens Watson's life, not just his own — **Lesson:** There are things worth more to him than the game itself — **Becomes:** Willing to fake his own death rather than risk his friend — **Status:** achieved

## WORLD
**Name:** Victorian London
**Type:** city
**Parent:** (leave blank)
**Note:** Fog, gas lamps, hansom cabs, and a criminal underworld dense enough to need a consulting detective at all.
**Worldbuilding:** |
  London in the 1880s and 1890s is a city of extremes — the same square mile holds Mycroft's gentleman's clubs and the opium dens of the docks, Scotland Yard's new detective bureau and the rookeries the police won't enter without an escort. Gas lamps make islands of light in a fog that can swallow a hansom cab whole; the Underground is young and half-finished; telegrams move faster than any person can. It's a city built, almost deliberately, for someone like Holmes: dense enough to hide anything, orderly enough on the surface that any real deviation stands out to a mind trained to see it. Watson's London is comfortable and a little weary — a doctor's-eye view of a city that never quite sleeps and never quite tells you everything at once.

## WORLD
**Name:** 221B Baker Street
**Type:** residence
**Parent:** Victorian London
**Note:** Holmes and Watson's shared lodgings, and the true stage for nearly every case in the canon.
**Worldbuilding:** |
  Two rooms up a narrow flight of seventeen steps from Mrs. Hudson's front hall: a shared sitting room and separate bedrooms. The sitting room is a study in productive chaos — a chemistry corner with acid-stained deal-topped tables, a mantelpiece where Holmes keeps his unanswered correspondence pinned with a jackknife, a Persian slipper stuffed with tobacco, and a coal-scuttle that (in later stories) holds his cigars. Bullet holes from an indoor bout of target practice mark a patriotic "VR" into the wallpaper. Books are piled by subject rather than shelved — Holmes's index of criminal cases and his monographs on cigar ash and tattoo marks share space with Watson's medical texts. The bow window looks down on Baker Street itself, useful for watching a client's cab arrive and reading their entire recent history before they've reached the door. It is, depending on the week, a laboratory, a shooting range, a case-file archive, and — for Watson especially — the only place that has ever felt fully like home.

## WORLD
**Name:** Scotland Yard
**Type:** institution
**Parent:** Victorian London
**Note:** The official face of London policing, perpetually a half-step behind the amateur genius working out of Baker Street.
**Worldbuilding:** |
  Scotland Yard represents order, procedure, and institutional caution — everything Holmes's method deliberately isn't. Its detectives, Lestrade chief among them, are neither fools nor villains; they're competent professionals working within rules, paperwork, and public accountability that Holmes simply ignores. The Yard needs Holmes precisely because it can't afford to operate the way he does — and resents needing him for exactly the same reason. Every case that reaches Baker Street through Scotland Yard's door is, structurally, an admission that the official method has already failed.

**Type:** private club
**Parent:** Victorian London
**Note:** A gentleman's club built around a single house rule: no member may speak to another, anywhere in the building, except in the visitors' room.
**Worldbuilding:** |
  Mycroft Holmes's natural habitat. The Diogenes Club exists for men who find other men's company tiring but still want somewhere comfortable to sit in it — silence is enforced, and a third infraction means expulsion. It is the perfect architecture for Mycroft: a place built to accommodate a towering intellect that simply cannot be bothered to exert itself socially, or often at all. Sherlock's visits here are rare and always mean the matter is serious enough to move his brother out of his armchair.

## WORLD
**Name:** Dartmoor / Baskerville Hall
**Type:** region
**Parent:** (leave blank)
**Note:** A vast, fog-bound, treacherous moorland in Devon, and the gothic manor at its heart where the Baskerville curse comes home to roost.
**Worldbuilding:** |
  Dartmoor is Holmes's London turned inside out — instead of dense streets and gaslight, it's open, empty, and dangerous precisely because there's nowhere to hide except the ground itself swallows you. The Grimpen Mire is the moor's most feared feature: a bog that can pull a misstepping pony under in minutes, marked by paths only a local — like Stapleton — could know well enough to cross safely. Baskerville Hall itself is a dark, ivy-choked manor with a yew alley where Sir Charles met his death, mullioned windows, and a family portrait gallery Holmes studies for a very particular reason. An escaped convict loose on the moor, ancient legend, isolated tenant farms, and a spectral hound heard howling in the fog make Dartmoor feel like a different genre entirely — Doyle deliberately writes it as gothic horror before Holmes's reasoning drags it back into the world of the explicable.

## WORLD
**Name:** Reichenbach Falls
**Type:** landmark
**Parent:** (leave blank)
**Note:** A towering waterfall in the Swiss Alps near Meiringen — the site of Holmes and Moriarty's final confrontation.
**Worldbuilding:** |
  A narrow path along the gorge, cut into the rock, leading to a viewing point where the water's roar makes conversation nearly impossible and a single misstep means the end. Doyle chose it as deliberately as any writer choosing a stage for a duel — remote enough that no rescue is plausible, dramatic enough that a fall reads as final. Watson's account has him lured away by a false errand, arriving back to find only two sets of footprints leading to the ledge and none leading away — and Holmes's note, left behind, explaining what he believes is about to happen. Whatever really occurred there, the falls become the single most consequential location in the entire canon.

## WORLD
**Name:** Lauriston Gardens
**Type:** district
**Parent:** Victorian London
**Note:** The site of the empty house where Holmes and Watson's first case together — the Drebber murder — begins.
**Worldbuilding:** |
  A dingy, half-suburban stretch off the Brixton Road with a handful of houses standing empty for lack of tenants. The scene of the crime that opens A Study in Scarlet: a locked, unfurnished house, a corpse with no visible wound, and the word RACHE written in blood on the wall. It's Holmes's first true showcase to Watson — and to the reader — of deduction performed on a genuinely blank scene, with almost nothing to go on but what everyone else has already dismissed as unremarkable.

## WORLD
**Name:** The Agra Fort
**Type:** landmark
**Parent:** (leave blank)
**Note:** A fortress in India where the Agra treasure was originally hidden — the distant origin point of The Sign of Four's central mystery.
**Worldbuilding:** |
  Referenced rather than visited on the page: the fort where, during the chaos of the Indian Mutiny, a fortune in jewels was hidden and then fought over by the men who found it — a debt and a betrayal that follows its survivors all the way back to London decades later. It functions as the mystery's origin myth, the distant "before" that explains everything happening in the present-day case.

## WORLD
**Name:** The Thames / Pool of London
**Type:** waterway
**Parent:** Victorian London
**Note:** London's working river, and the setting for one of the canon's most famous chase sequences.
**Worldbuilding:** |
  Crowded with barges, steam launches, and cargo traffic, the Thames through the Pool of London is as much a highway as any street — and one Holmes uses to devastating effect when he commandeers a police launch to run down Jonathan Small's boat, the Aurora, in a chase through fog and river traffic that closes out The Sign of Four's central mystery.
