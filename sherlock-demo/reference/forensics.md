## ENTRY
**Category:** Trace evidence
**Name:** Fingerprint identification
**Description:** Using the unique ridge patterns on a person's fingertips to place them at a scene or match them to a print left on an object.
**Examples:**
  - **Work:** The Sign of Four — **Medium:** Novel (Arthur Conan Doyle) — **Year:** 1890 — **Quality:** weak —
    **Text:** Doyle wrote before fingerprint identification was standard forensic practice in Britain (Scotland Yard didn't adopt it until 1901); Holmes relies instead on footprints, tobacco ash, and physical build. Useful as a reminder that the Holmes canon predates the technology now considered a genre baseline — a writer setting a case in this period should treat fingerprinting as cutting-edge or not-yet-arrived, not assumed.
  - **Work:** The Naval Treaty — **Medium:** Short story (Arthur Conan Doyle) — **Year:** 1893 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Though not using fingerprints specifically, Holmes's insistence on examining physical trace evidence at a scene before anyone else contaminates it — footprints in mud, a windowsill's marks — anticipates the crime-scene-integrity principle that modern fingerprint and forensic collection depends on entirely.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Chemical & trace analysis
**Name:** Poison and toxicology detection
**Description:** Identifying a poison used in a crime through its chemical signature, physiological effects, or residue.
**Examples:**
  - **Work:** A Study in Scarlet — **Medium:** Novel (Arthur Conan Doyle) — **Year:** 1887 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Holmes's introduction to the reader includes his own invented test for detecting bloodstains, described as more sensitive than the guaiacum test actually in use at the time — Doyle giving his detective a fictional edge over real 1880s forensic chemistry that makes the character feel ahead of his era rather than merely competent within it.
  - **Work:** The Poisonwood Bible — **Medium:** Novel (Barbara Kingsolver) — **Year:** 1998 — **Quality:** weak —
    **Text:** Not a detective novel, but useful as a negative example: the book treats poison symbolically rather than procedurally, with no interest in detection or diagnosis. Included here as a reminder that "poison in fiction" and "poison as a detective-fiction clue" are different craft problems — the latter requires the poison's effects and detection method to be internally consistent enough for a fair-play reveal.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Trace evidence
**Name:** Ballistics and bullet matching
**Description:** Matching a fired bullet or cartridge case to the specific firearm that fired it, via the unique marks a barrel leaves on a round.
**Examples:**
  - **Work:** The Valley of Fear — **Medium:** Novel (Arthur Conan Doyle) — **Year:** 1915 — **Quality:** weak —
    **Text:** Written just as comparative ballistics was becoming a real forensic discipline (Calvin Goddard's comparison microscope work was still years away), Doyle's gun evidence in this novel stays largely at the level of "a shotgun was used" rather than any individualized weapon matching — a useful marker of the technique's real historical timeline for writers setting cases in this exact period.
  - **Work:** The Silkworm — **Medium:** Novel (Robert Galbraith / J.K. Rowling) — **Year:** 2014 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** A modern private-detective novel that uses forensic ballistics as one thread among several physical-evidence types, treated with procedural specificity rather than hand-waved — a useful contemporary contrast to Doyle's era-appropriate vagueness.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Trace evidence
**Name:** Handwriting analysis
**Description:** Examining the physical characteristics of handwriting — pressure, slant, letterforms — to identify a writer or detect forgery.
**Examples:**
  - **Work:** The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes ("A Case of Identity") — **Medium:** Short story (Arthur Conan Doyle) — **Year:** 1891 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Holmes identifies that a typewritten letter's mechanical quirks (a slightly bent letter, uneven spacing) function like handwriting — an early and genuinely clever extension of document examination to a machine rather than a hand, years before typewriter identification became a recognized forensic subfield.
  - **Work:** Gone Girl — **Medium:** Novel (Gillian Flynn) — **Year:** 2012 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Amy's forged diary entries, written left-handed to disguise her natural right-handed script, play directly on the assumption that handwriting is a reliable, hard-to-fake identifier — the plot's later reveal depends on the reader having accepted that assumption along with the police.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Trace evidence
**Name:** Photography as evidence
**Description:** Using photographs — of a scene, a person, or an object — as documentary proof in an investigation.
**Examples:**
  - **Work:** A Scandal in Bohemia — **Medium:** Short story (Arthur Conan Doyle) — **Year:** 1891 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** The entire plot turns on a single incriminating photograph of Irene Adler and the King of Bohemia — notable because photography here isn't used for scene documentation (the technique's later, more common forensic role) but as blackmail material, treating the photograph itself as the central object of the mystery rather than a tool for solving one.
  - **Work:** Blow-Up — **Medium:** Film (Michelangelo Antonioni) — **Year:** 1966 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** A photographer enlarges his own photos and gradually believes he's captured evidence of a murder — using photographic evidence as a mystery's central epistemological question (can you trust what the photograph shows once you've enlarged it past the point of clarity?) rather than a straightforward clue.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Investigation method
**Name:** Surveillance and shadowing
**Description:** Covertly following or observing a suspect to gather information without their knowledge.
**Examples:**
  - **Work:** The Hound of the Baskervilles — **Medium:** Novel (Arthur Conan Doyle) — **Year:** 1902 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Holmes secretly relocates to a stone hut on Dartmoor to surveil the case from hiding while letting Watson (and the reader) believe he's still in London — a rare structural use of surveillance where the detective is shadowing the investigation itself, and the reveal of his presence functions as a plot twist rather than only an investigative technique.
  - **Work:** The Conversation — **Medium:** Film (Francis Ford Coppola) — **Year:** 1974 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Centers an entire film on a professional surveillance expert and the ethical and psychological toll of his own eavesdropping work — a useful counter-model to Doyle's brisk, consequence-free deployment of the technique, showing surveillance can be the emotional core of a mystery rather than just a plot mechanism.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Trace evidence
**Name:** Blood spatter analysis
**Description:** Reading the shape, size, and distribution of bloodstains at a scene to reconstruct how a violent act occurred.
**Examples:**
  - **Work:** Dexter — **Medium:** TV series — **Year:** 2006-2013 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Built around a protagonist who is himself a blood-spatter analyst for the Miami police, the show treats the discipline with sustained procedural specificity across its run — a strong model for a writer wanting a detective character whose forensic specialty is also their defining professional identity, not a one-scene skill.
  - **Work:** CSI: Crime Scene Investigation — **Medium:** TV series — **Year:** 2000-2015 — **Quality:** weak —
    **Text:** Frequently cited by real forensic scientists as an example of "the CSI effect" — dramatizing blood spatter and other trace analysis as faster, more certain, and more visually conclusive than real lab work actually is, to the point of measurably affecting real jury expectations in criminal trials. Useful as a caution against overselling forensic certainty for dramatic convenience.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Investigation method
**Name:** Lock-picking and forced entry analysis
**Description:** Either bypassing a lock covertly, or examining a point of entry afterward to determine how (and whether) it was forced.
**Examples:**
  - **Work:** The Red-Headed League — **Medium:** Short story (Arthur Conan Doyle) — **Year:** 1891 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Holmes deduces an entire bank-vault tunneling scheme from a pawnbroker's oddly knee-worn trousers, without ever needing to examine a lock directly — a reminder that forced-entry analysis in fiction doesn't have to be about the lock itself; the physical evidence of the *effort* of entry (dust, wear, fatigue) can carry the same clue.
  - **Work:** Ocean's Eleven — **Medium:** Film (Steven Soderbergh) — **Year:** 2001 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Uses lock-picking and vault-bypass as heist spectacle from the criminal's side rather than an investigator's — useful contrast for a writer deciding whether to show the technique as an investigative deduction after the fact (Doyle's approach) or as a real-time process the audience watches unfold (the heist-film approach).

## ENTRY
**Category:** Chemical & trace analysis
**Name:** Cipher-breaking and cryptanalysis
**Description:** Decoding an encrypted or disguised message using logical and statistical methods.
**Examples:**
  - **Work:** The Adventure of the Dancing Men — **Medium:** Short story (Arthur Conan Doyle) — **Year:** 1903 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Holmes cracks a stick-figure substitution cipher using frequency analysis — the letter E is the most common in English, so the most frequent symbol likely represents it — a real cryptanalytic technique presented accurately enough that a careful reader could actually replicate the method.
  - **Work:** The Imitation Game — **Medium:** Film (Morten Tyldum) — **Year:** 2014 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Dramatizes the real Bletchley Park effort to break the Enigma cipher during WWII — a large-scale, team-based, machine-assisted version of the same fundamental problem Holmes solves by hand and alone, useful as a scale contrast for how cryptanalysis evolved from an individual deductive skill into an industrial one.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Chemical & trace analysis
**Name:** Trace chemistry (soil, ash, and fiber identification)
**Description:** Identifying a person's recent movements or occupation from microscopic physical residue — cigar ash, soil type, fabric fibers — carried on their body or clothing.
**Examples:**
  - **Work:** The complete Sherlock Holmes canon — **Medium:** Novels & short stories (Arthur Conan Doyle) — **Year:** 1887-1927 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Holmes is stated to have written a monograph distinguishing 140 varieties of tobacco ash by sight — a fictional level of granularity beyond any real forensic capability of the period, but the underlying principle (that trace residue carries identifiable, specific information) anticipates real trace-evidence science by decades.
  - **Work:** Mindhunter — **Medium:** TV series — **Year:** 2017-2019 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Focuses more on behavioral profiling than trace chemistry specifically, but its grounded, research-based treatment of physical evidence collection is a useful modern contrast to Holmes's near-magical trace-reading — showing how real trace evidence work is slower, more collaborative, and more uncertain than the genre's classic detective genius model.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Modern forensics
**Name:** DNA analysis
**Description:** Using genetic material recovered from a scene to identify or exclude a suspect with statistical certainty.
**Examples:**
  - **Work:** In the Woods — **Medium:** Novel (Tana French) — **Year:** 2007 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** French's Dublin Murder Squad novels treat DNA and modern forensic procedure as routine background reality rather than a dramatic reveal moment — a useful model for a contemporary-set mystery, where the technology's existence changes what kinds of mysteries are even plausible (many classic Golden Age plots simply don't work once DNA evidence is assumed available).
  - **Work:** CSI: Crime Scene Investigation — **Medium:** TV series — **Year:** 2000-2015 — **Quality:** weak —
    **Text:** Routinely compresses DNA analysis turnaround from the real-world days-to-weeks into on-screen minutes — another instance of the "CSI effect," worth flagging again specifically for DNA since it's the forensic technique most affected by unrealistic on-screen speed in popular perception.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Modern forensics
**Name:** CCTV and digital surveillance
**Description:** Using recorded camera footage, location data, or other passive digital records to place a suspect at a scene or reconstruct a timeline.
**Examples:**
  - **Work:** Zodiac — **Medium:** Film (David Fincher) — **Year:** 2007 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Set in an era just before ubiquitous digital surveillance, the film's investigators are hobbled by exactly the kind of evidence a modern case would have (traffic cameras, phone records) and don't — a useful illustration of how much detective-fiction plotting has had to adapt to the sheer volume of passive digital evidence now generated by ordinary life.
  - **Work:** Sherlock ("A Study in Pink") — **Medium:** TV episode (Steven Moffat & Mark Gatiss) — **Year:** 2010 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Modernizes Holmes's deductive method to explicitly include phone GPS data and CCTV as tools alongside physical observation — a direct, self-aware translation exercise for a writer wondering how a Doyle-style deductive detective's toolkit should update for a contemporary setting.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Modern forensics
**Name:** Wiretapping and communications interception
**Description:** Covertly intercepting a suspect's phone calls or other communications as an investigative technique.
**Examples:**
  - **Work:** The Wire — **Medium:** TV series — **Year:** 2002-2008 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Built an entire season's structure around the legal and procedural mechanics of obtaining and using a wiretap against a drug organization — arguably the most procedurally accurate popular depiction of the technique, treating the bureaucratic difficulty of getting a wiretap approved as real dramatic material in itself, not just a background detail.
  - **Work:** The Conversation — **Medium:** Film (Francis Ford Coppola) — **Year:** 1974 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Again useful here specifically for its focus on the wiretapper's own paranoia and ethical unraveling — a reminder that the technique can be dramatized from the interceptor's interior experience, not only from the perspective of the detective using the intercepted material.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Modern forensics
**Name:** Digital forensics
**Description:** Recovering and analyzing evidence from computers, phones, or other digital devices — deleted files, metadata, browsing history, location logs.
**Examples:**
  - **Work:** Mare of Easttown — **Medium:** TV series — **Year:** 2021 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Weaves phone records and social media evidence into a small-town procedural without letting the digital evidence do all the investigative work itself — the human interview and community-knowledge threads remain load-bearing, a useful balance for a writer worried digital forensics can make older-style deductive investigation feel obsolete.
  - **Work:** Serial (podcast, Season 1) — **Medium:** Podcast (Sarah Koenig) — **Year:** 2014 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** A real-case journalistic investigation built substantially around the reliability (and unreliability) of 1999-era cell tower location data — a genuinely instructive real-world case study in exactly how uncertain "digital forensic evidence" can be even when it looks authoritative, useful grounding for any writer tempted to treat digital evidence as infallible.
