## ENTRY
**Category:** Trace evidence
**Name:** Fingerprint identification
**Description:** Using the unique ridge patterns on a person's fingertips to place them at a scene or match them to a print left on an object.
**Examples:**
  - **Work:** The Sign of Four — **Medium:** Novel (Arthur Conan Doyle) — **Year:** 1890 — **Quality:** weak —
    **Text:** Doyle wrote before fingerprint identification was standard forensic practice in Britain (Scotland Yard didn't adopt it until 1901); Holmes relies instead on footprints, tobacco ash, and physical build. Useful as a reminder that the Holmes canon predates the technology now considered a genre baseline — a writer setting a case in this period should treat fingerprinting as cutting-edge or not-yet-arrived, not assumed.
  - **Work:** The Naval Treaty — **Medium:** Short story (Arthur Conan Doyle) — **Year:** 1893 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Though not using fingerprints specifically, Holmes's insistence on examining physical trace evidence at a scene before anyone else contaminates it — footprints in mud, a windowsill's marks — anticipates the crime-scene-integrity principle that modern fingerprint and forensic collection depends on entirely.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Chemical & trace analysis
**Name:** Poison and toxicology detection
**Description:** Identifying a poison used in a crime through its chemical signature, physiological effects, or residue.
**Examples:**
  - **Work:** A Study in Scarlet — **Medium:** Novel (Arthur Conan Doyle) — **Year:** 1887 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Holmes's introduction to the reader includes his own invented test for detecting bloodstains, described as more sensitive than the guaiacum test actually in use at the time — Doyle giving his detective a fictional edge over real 1880s forensic chemistry that makes the character feel ahead of his era rather than merely competent within it.
  - **Work:** The Poisonwood Bible — **Medium:** Novel (Barbara Kingsolver) — **Year:** 1998 — **Quality:** weak —
    **Text:** Not a detective novel, but useful as a negative example: the book treats poison symbolically rather than procedurally, with no interest in detection or diagnosis. Included here as a reminder that "poison in fiction" and "poison as a detective-fiction clue" are different craft problems — the latter requires the poison's effects and detection method to be internally consistent enough for a fair-play reveal.
