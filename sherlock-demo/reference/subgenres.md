## ENTRY
**Category:** Core modes
**Name:** Golden Age fair-play mystery
**Description:** The puzzle-box tradition where every clue is shown to the reader before the reveal.
**Principle:** |
  Flourished roughly 1920-1950, dominated by British writers (Christie, Sayers, Marsh, Allingham — sometimes called the "Queens of Crime"). The genre's implicit contract: the reader has, in principle, everything needed to solve the case before the detective explains it. Plot puzzle takes precedence over psychological depth or social realism; the closed-circle setting (country house, train, island) keeps the suspect pool manageable.
**Example:** |
  Ronald Knox's 1929 "Ten Commandments of Detective Fiction" codified the era's fair-play rules explicitly — no undisclosed twins, no unknown poisons, no last-minute confessions from characters never previously introduced — a genuinely useful checklist for any writer working in this mode today.
**Application:** |
  Choose this mode when the pleasure you want the reader to have is the game itself — matching wits with the author. Requires rigorous clue-planting discipline: every fact the detective uses in the reveal must have been visible to the reader earlier, even if its significance wasn't.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Core modes
**Name:** Hardboiled / noir
**Description:** The morally compromised private eye moving through a corrupt city he can't fix, only survive.
**Principle:** |
  Emerged in 1920s-40s America (Hammett, Chandler, and the pulp magazine Black Mask), largely as a reaction against the Golden Age's genteel puzzle-box mode. The detective is often as morally ambiguous as the criminals; the case is frequently an excuse to tour a corrupt social landscape rather than a clean logical puzzle. Prose style — terse, metaphor-driven, voice-forward — carries as much weight as plot.
**Example:** |
  Raymond Chandler's 1944 essay "The Simple Art of Murder" is the genre's own manifesto, explicitly attacking the fair-play puzzle tradition as bloodless and arguing crime fiction should be about "a real world, a world in which gangsters can rule nations."
**Application:** |
  Choose this mode when the book wants moral weight and atmosphere over airtight puzzle logic — a detective changed or worn down by the case, rather than one who solves it and walks away unbothered. Plot looseness is a stylistic choice here, not a flaw, but only works if the reader isn't expecting Christie-style fairness.
