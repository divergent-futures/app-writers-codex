## ENTRY
**Category:** Core modes
**Name:** Golden Age fair-play mystery
**Description:** The puzzle-box tradition where every clue is shown to the reader before the reveal.
**Principle:** |
  Flourished roughly 1920-1950, dominated by British writers (Christie, Sayers, Marsh, Allingham — sometimes called the "Queens of Crime"). The genre's implicit contract: the reader has, in principle, everything needed to solve the case before the detective explains it. Plot puzzle takes precedence over psychological depth or social realism; the closed-circle setting (country house, train, island) keeps the suspect pool manageable.
**Example:** |
  Ronald Knox's 1929 "Ten Commandments of Detective Fiction" codified the era's fair-play rules explicitly — no undisclosed twins, no unknown poisons, no last-minute confessions from characters never previously introduced — a genuinely useful checklist for any writer working in this mode today.
**Application:** |
  Choose this mode when the pleasure you want the reader to have is the game itself — matching wits with the author. Requires rigorous clue-planting discipline: every fact the detective uses in the reveal must have been visible to the reader earlier, even if its significance wasn't.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Core modes
**Name:** Hardboiled / noir
**Description:** The morally compromised private eye moving through a corrupt city he can't fix, only survive.
**Principle:** |
  Emerged in 1920s-40s America (Hammett, Chandler, and the pulp magazine Black Mask), largely as a reaction against the Golden Age's genteel puzzle-box mode. The detective is often as morally ambiguous as the criminals; the case is frequently an excuse to tour a corrupt social landscape rather than a clean logical puzzle. Prose style — terse, metaphor-driven, voice-forward — carries as much weight as plot.
**Example:** |
  Raymond Chandler's 1944 essay "The Simple Art of Murder" is the genre's own manifesto, explicitly attacking the fair-play puzzle tradition as bloodless and arguing crime fiction should be about "a real world, a world in which gangsters can rule nations."
**Application:** |
  Choose this mode when the book wants moral weight and atmosphere over airtight puzzle logic — a detective changed or worn down by the case, rather than one who solves it and walks away unbothered. Plot looseness is a stylistic choice here, not a flaw, but only works if the reader isn't expecting Christie-style fairness.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Core modes
**Name:** Cozy mystery
**Description:** Low-violence, community-centered mysteries, typically with an amateur sleuth and a warm, small-scale setting.
**Principle:** |
  Violence happens off the page or is minimally described; the emotional register stays light even when the crime is serious. The detective is usually an amateur embedded in a tight-knit community (a village, a bookshop, a bakery) whose local knowledge and relationships, not forensic training, drive the investigation. Miss Marple's village mysteries are the genre's clearest ancestor.
**Example:** |
  Modern cozy series (Richard Osman's The Thursday Murder Club, 2020, is a recent bestselling example) typically build the amateur-sleuth ensemble around a shared physical space or hobby, letting recurring side characters carry as much of the reader's attachment as the mystery plot itself.
**Application:** |
  Choose this mode for a series meant to be comforting as much as suspenseful — readers return for the cast and setting between cases as much as for any individual puzzle. Keep violence implied rather than graphic, and give the amateur detective a plausible, repeatable reason to keep encountering new crimes.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Core modes
**Name:** Police procedural
**Description:** A mystery grounded in the real institutional machinery of professional law enforcement — chain of custody, jurisdiction, bureaucracy, teamwork.
**Principle:** |
  Unlike the lone amateur genius (Holmes) or private eye (Marlowe), the procedural's detective operates inside an institution with its own rules, politics, and limitations — warrants must be obtained, evidence must be handled correctly to be admissible, and a case can be lost on a technicality even when the detective is right. The institution itself often becomes a character.
**Example:** |
  Georges Simenon's Maigret novels (from 1931) are an early European example; Ed McBain's 87th Precinct novels (from 1956) popularized the ensemble-squad version in America, following a whole department rather than one star detective.
**Application:** |
  Choose this mode for realism and institutional texture — useful when the story wants to explore how bureaucracy, resource limits, or departmental politics shape (or distort) the pursuit of a case, not just the detective's individual brilliance.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Core modes
**Name:** Locked-room / impossible crime
**Description:** A crime that appears physically impossible to have been committed, given the constraints of the scene.
**Principle:** |
  The subgenre's entire pleasure is structural: the detective (and reader) must find the flaw in an apparently airtight physical impossibility — a sealed room, footprints that stop in open snow, a murder witnessed by several people who all swear no one else was present. John Dickson Carr is the form's acknowledged master.
**Example:** |
  Carr's The Hollow Man (1935) is widely voted the genre's best example, and includes a chapter where the detective directly lectures the reader on the taxonomy of locked-room solution types — a rare moment of a genre openly showing its own toolkit.
**Application:** |
  Choose this mode when the puzzle itself, as pure mechanism, is the point — readers of this subgenre specifically want to be baffled by a physical impossibility and then delighted by an elegant, fair, physically sound explanation.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Adjacent modes
**Name:** Legal thriller
**Description:** A mystery or suspense plot built around courtroom procedure, legal strategy, or a case being tried rather than only investigated.
**Principle:** |
  The tension shifts partly from "who did it" to "can it be proven, admissibly, within the rules of a trial" — a guilty person can walk free on a technicality, and an innocent person can be convicted on circumstantial evidence, giving the subgenre a different kind of suspense than pure detection.
**Example:** |
  Erle Stanley Gardner's Perry Mason novels (from 1933) are an early, hugely popular example, with the courtroom cross-examination itself functioning as the detective's investigative set piece.
**Application:** |
  Choose this mode when you want the climax to be a trial or hearing rather than a private reveal — the "gathered suspects" drawing-room scene's institutional cousin, with a judge and jury standing in for the assembled suspects.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Adjacent modes
**Name:** Forensic thriller
**Description:** A mystery where forensic science itself — the lab, the autopsy, the analysis — is the investigative centerpiece rather than a supporting tool.
**Principle:** |
  Emerged largely from the late 20th century onward, as real forensic science (DNA, digital forensics) became both more powerful and more publicly understood. The detective is often a forensic specialist themselves (a medical examiner, a crime-scene analyst) rather than a police detective in the traditional sense.
**Example:** |
  Patricia Cornwell's Kay Scarpetta novels (from 1990) center a chief medical examiner as protagonist, treating autopsy and lab procedure with sustained technical detail as the plot's driving investigative method.
**Application:** |
  Choose this mode when you want physical, scientific evidence — not interviews or psychological insight — to be the primary engine of discovery. Watch the "CSI effect" risk: real forensic turnaround and certainty is slower and messier than the subgenre's dramatic conventions often suggest.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Adjacent modes
**Name:** Historical mystery
**Description:** A mystery set in a clearly researched past era, where the historical setting itself shapes what investigative methods and social constraints are available.
**Principle:** |
  The detective must solve the case using only period-appropriate technology and knowledge — no fingerprinting before roughly 1901, no DNA ever before the 1980s, no telephones before the 1870s — which the genre often treats as a puzzle constraint in itself, not just set dressing.
**Example:** |
  Umberto Eco's The Name of the Rose (1980), set in a 14th-century monastery, and Josephine Tey's The Daughter of Time (1951), investigating a 15th-century royal murder from a hospital bed, represent two very different registers of the subgenre — one immersive and world-built, one analytical and document-based.
**Application:** |
  Choose this mode when period accuracy is itself part of the appeal — but audit your solution against what was actually possible for the era; an anachronistic forensic technique breaks the subgenre's specific contract even if it wouldn't matter in a contemporary-set story.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Adjacent modes
**Name:** Psychological suspense
**Description:** A mystery driven primarily by a character's interior unraveling, unreliable perception, or moral compromise, rather than external clue-gathering.
**Principle:** |
  The reader's uncertainty is often about the narrator's own reliability or sanity, not just about who committed the crime — the tension comes from not being sure how much of what's being narrated is true. Gillian Flynn's Gone Girl (2012) is a defining modern example, alternating two narrators whose accounts directly contradict each other.
**Example:** |
  Patricia Highsmith's The Talented Mr. Ripley (1955) inverts the mode from detective's perspective to criminal's, sustaining suspense from the reader's uneasy sympathy for a murderer rather than from any detective's pursuit.
**Application:** |
  Choose this mode when the story's real question is psychological rather than procedural — the reader should finish uncertain what to feel about a character's actions, not just satisfied that a puzzle was solved.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Structure
**Name:** Inverted mystery (howcatchem)
**Description:** The audience knows the crime and culprit from the outset; the tension is in watching the detective figure out what the audience already knows.
**Principle:** |
  Inverts the genre's usual central question — instead of "who did it," the question becomes "how will they be caught, and what will finally trip them up." Requires the culprit to be an interesting enough character to sustain audience attention even without mystery about their guilt.
**Example:** |
  Columbo (1968-2003) is the form's defining example: nearly every episode opens by showing the murder in full, then spends the runtime on the detective's patient accumulation of the one detail that breaks the killer's plan.
**Application:** |
  Choose this mode when you want to explore a killer's psychology directly, or when the mechanics of *getting caught* are more interesting than the mystery of *who did it* — works especially well for a detective whose investigative style (patient, deceptively casual, methodical) is itself the draw.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Adjacent modes
**Name:** Country house mystery
**Description:** A mystery confined to an isolated estate with a small, closed cast of suspects — the classic setting of the Golden Age era.
**Principle:** |
  A specific, highly codified variant of the closed-circle structure: a house party, a will reading, or a weekend gathering brings a fixed cast together in a setting cut off (by distance, weather, or social convention) from outside interference, making the suspect pool small and stable enough to sustain a rigorous fair-play puzzle.
**Example:** |
  Agatha Christie used the form repeatedly (among many examples, The Mysterious Affair at Styles, 1920, her debut novel), and it remains popular enough to be actively revived — Knives Out (2019) restages the convention with a modern, self-aware sensibility.
**Application:** |
  Choose this mode when you want the setting itself to do structural work — limiting the suspect pool, controlling information flow, and giving the detective a physically bounded space to investigate methodically, room by room, in a way an open city setting doesn't allow.
