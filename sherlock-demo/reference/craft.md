## ENTRY
**Category:** The fair-play contract
**Name:** Clue visibility and the fair-play rule
**Description:** Every fact the detective uses in the final reveal must have been shown to the reader earlier, even if its significance wasn't clear at the time.
**Principle:** |
  The reader should be able to look back after the reveal and find every clue in the text — not necessarily understood, but present. A solution built on information withheld from the reader (an unmentioned twin, an undisclosed poison, a fact revealed only in the final chapter) breaks the genre's central promise and reads as a cheat, however clever it seems to the writer.
**Example:** |
  Ronald Knox's 1929 "Ten Commandments" and the "Detection Club" oath he helped formalize among Golden Age writers (including Christie and Sayers) were explicit attempts to codify this rule after readers began complaining about unfair mysteries — a genre self-regulating its own contract with its audience.
**Application:** |
  When drafting a reveal, work backward: list every fact the detective states in the denouement, then check each one appears on the page earlier. If a fact appears for the first time in the reveal itself, either plant it earlier or cut it from the solution.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Misdirection
**Name:** Constructing an honest red herring
**Description:** A false lead that misdirects the reader without lying to them — built from selective emphasis, not fabricated evidence.
**Principle:** |
  A red herring works by giving a suspect a real motive, a real opportunity, and a plausible-looking piece of evidence — then letting the reader's own assumptions do the misdirecting. The trick is emphasis, not deception: everything shown about the false suspect should be true, just incomplete or interpreted the way the reader naturally would.
**Example:** |
  Christie's method, most visible in Murder on the Orient Express, is to give nearly every character on the page a genuine, independently sufficient motive — so the red herrings aren't fake clues at all, just true ones that happen to point at the wrong (or, famously in that book, an insufficiently singular) conclusion.
**Application:** |
  Give your false suspect the same density of real, checkable detail you give your real culprit. A red herring built on a lie the narrative later has to retract feels like a cheat; a red herring built on true facts arranged to invite the wrong conclusion feels like fair play, even in hindsight.

**Category:** Structure
**Name:** Staging the gathered-suspects reveal
**Description:** How to run the drawing-room denouement scene so it lands as a satisfying performance rather than a dry recap.
**Principle:** |
  The scene works when the detective isn't just reciting facts already known to the reader but actively performing the *connections* between them for the first time — the pleasure is watching separate, previously inert clues suddenly click into a single structure. Pacing matters: reveal the mechanism before the culprit's name where possible, so the audience gets one more beat of "so who does this point to?" before the final unmasking.
**Example:** |
  Poirot characteristically walks the room through several suspects' possible guilt in turn before naming the true culprit, deliberately keeping multiple people plausible for as long as possible rather than moving in a straight line to the answer — sustaining suspense even though the reader already has every clue in hand.
**Application:** |
  Draft the reveal scene as a sequence of small individual realizations, not one large exposition dump — let the detective (and reader) re-examine two or three specific suspects' apparent alibis or motives in turn before the final one collapses, so the scene has its own internal rising action.

**Category:** Pacing
**Name:** Foreshadowing without tipping the hand
**Description:** Planting a clue so it reads as incidental detail on first pass but becomes obviously significant in hindsight.
**Principle:** |
  The best-planted clues are attached to something else the scene is already doing — a piece of dialogue that's ostensibly about character or atmosphere, with the actual clue riding along inside it, so the reader's attention isn't drawn to it as A Clue. A detail introduced with unusual narrative emphasis (a full paragraph of description, a repeated mention) signals its own importance and tips the reader off too early.
**Example:** |
  Chekhov's general dramatic principle — a gun shown on the wall in Act One must go off by Act Three — is the blunt version; fair-play mystery craft refines it further, since a mystery's "gun" needs to be visible but not *emphasized*, so the reader notices it only in retrospect.
**Application:** |
  A useful test: read the scene containing your planted clue and ask whether it would survive being cut for pacing reasons if you didn't know it was secretly load-bearing. If the scene clearly exists only to deliver the clue, the clue is over-signposted; if the clue could plausibly be trimmed as incidental color, it's well hidden.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Pacing
**Name:** Pacing an investigation's beat structure
**Description:** How to sequence a case's escalating stages — the initial puzzle, the false lead, the complication, the breakthrough — so the middle of a mystery doesn't sag.
**Principle:** |
  A well-paced investigation typically escalates through a small number of distinct movements: an initial hook (the crime, or its discovery), an early false lead that seems promising, a complication that raises the stakes or narrows the timeline, and a breakthrough that reframes everything before the final reveal. Investigations that simply accumulate clue after clue at a flat rate tend to sag in the middle third.
**Example:** |
  Golden Age novels frequently insert a second crime (an attempted murder, a suspicious "suicide," a witness silenced) partway through specifically to reset the reader's energy and raise stakes right at the point the initial mystery's momentum would otherwise flag.
**Application:** |
  If a draft's middle section feels slow, consider whether it needs a complication beat — not necessarily a second crime, but some event that meaningfully changes the shape of the investigation (a suspect flees, an alibi collapses, new evidence contradicts the leading theory) rather than simply adding one more clue to the pile.

**Category:** Reasoning on the page
**Name:** Writing a deduction chain the reader can follow
**Description:** How to render a detective's leap from observation to conclusion so it feels earned rather than magical.
**Principle:** |
  A satisfying deduction chain is explicit about its intermediate steps, even when the detective delivers it quickly — "the mud on your boots is a specific reddish clay found only near the quarry, therefore you were there recently" is legible; "I deduced you'd been to the quarry" with no shown reasoning is not. The reader doesn't need to have spotted the clue themselves, but needs to be able to verify the logic once it's shown.
**Example:** |
  Holmes's own deductions are almost always delivered with their reasoning chain intact, even in throwaway character-introduction moments ("Wedlock suits you... you have been getting yourself very wet lately, and... a most clumsy and careless servant girl") — Doyle rarely lets Holmes simply assert a conclusion without walking through the physical evidence behind it.
**Application:** |
  When drafting a deduction, write the full reasoning chain first, then decide how much to compress on the page — cutting steps for pace is fine, but the underlying logic should always be reconstructable, even if the detective delivers the compressed version aloud.

**Category:** Structure
**Name:** Structuring the least-likely-suspect reveal
**Description:** How to make a surprising culprit feel inevitable in hindsight rather than arbitrary.
**Principle:** |
  The least-likely suspect needs the same amount of real, checkable groundwork as any other suspect — a genuine motive established early, real opportunity, and at least one piece of evidence that, properly read, actually points at them, even if the reader (and other characters) initially read it as pointing elsewhere or as insignificant. The "surprise" should survive a second reading; if it only works because information was hidden, it isn't a fair least-likely-suspect reveal, just a twist.
**Example:** |
  The Murder of Roger Ackroyd's culprit reveal is still debated precisely because it tests this boundary — Christie's defenders argue every physical clue about the narrator's guilt was genuinely shown, while critics argue the narrator's *omissions* function too close to an outright lie to the reader.
**Application:** |
  Give your least-likely suspect a small, genuine "tell" scene early in the draft — a moment where their behavior is slightly off in a way that's easy to explain away on first read but reads as damning once the reader knows the truth. Test it by asking whether a sharp-eyed reader could plausibly have flagged them on a first pass, not just on a reread.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Dialogue
**Name:** Writing believable interrogation dialogue
**Description:** How to write a questioning scene so it generates real tension and information without becoming a flat Q&A transcript.
**Principle:** |
  A good interrogation scene has both parties working an agenda — the detective probing for inconsistency, the suspect (guilty or not) managing their own self-presentation, whether from guilt, fear, grief, or simple irritation at being suspected. Real information should emerge sideways, through what a character avoids saying or how they react to a specific question, not only through direct answers.
**Example:** |
  Columbo's interrogation scenes are built almost entirely around this principle — the detective's deliberately unthreatening, scattered demeanor lulls the guilty party into a false sense of control, so their own overconfidence, not any aggressive questioning, produces the fatal slip.
**Application:** |
  Before drafting an interrogation scene, decide what each character wants from it beyond the surface question-and-answer — the detective's real goal (confirm a specific suspicion, provoke a reaction, buy time) and the suspect's real goal (appear cooperative, protect someone else, simply survive the conversation) should shape the scene's actual dialogue, not just its factual content.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Pacing
**Name:** The trail-of-breadcrumbs clue-planting cadence
**Description:** How frequently and evenly to distribute clues across a manuscript so the ending feels earned rather than either over-signposted or a total surprise.
**Principle:** |
  Clues distributed in a steady, spaced rhythm across the manuscript — rather than clustered in one "clue chapter" or all crammed into the final act — let a careful reader assemble the solution gradually, which is part of the genre's core pleasure, while a less attentive reader still experiences the ending as a satisfying surprise rather than an obvious inevitability telegraphed too early.
**Example:** |
  Christie characteristically spaces her key clues across multiple, tonally different scenes (a piece of dialogue in one chapter, a physical detail in another, an offhand remark from a minor character much later) so no single scene reads as "the clue scene," making the full pattern hard to spot without real attention.
**Application:** |
  Map your manuscript's planted clues on a simple list against chapter numbers once a draft exists — if they cluster too heavily in one section, or all sit in the final quarter, redistribute some earlier so the fair-play contract holds without making the solution obvious well before the reveal.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Structure
**Name:** Balancing procedural detail against pacing
**Description:** How much real forensic, legal, or investigative process detail to include before it starts slowing the story down.
**Principle:** |
  Procedural detail earns its place when it's doing double duty — advancing the plot or characterizing the investigator — rather than existing purely to demonstrate research. A general guideline: include enough specific, accurate process detail to establish credibility and texture, but cut or compress the parts that don't change what the reader knows or feels.
**Example:** |
  Tana French's Dublin Murder Squad novels are frequently praised for procedural authenticity without procedural bloat — routine steps (canvassing witnesses, filing paperwork) are compressed or summarized, while the specific interviews or evidence that actually move the plot get full scene treatment.
**Application:** |
  When drafting a procedural scene, ask whether the reader needs to see this specific step happen, or just needs to know it happened — a line like "the forensics team spent three days on the scene" can often replace a full scene of forensics work that isn't independently revealing new information.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Structure
**Name:** Writing a satisfying "how" after establishing "who"
**Description:** In an inverted mystery (howcatchem) or any structure where the culprit is known early, the mechanism of eventual discovery needs its own dramatic shape.
**Principle:** |
  Once the "who" is off the table, tension has to come from somewhere else — usually the gap between the culprit's confidence in their plan and the small, specific detail that will eventually undo it. That detail should be established early (so it's fair to the audience) but not made obviously significant, so its return late in the story lands as a genuine discovery rather than the audience simply waiting for what they already know is coming.
**Example:** |
  Columbo's structure almost always establishes the eventual "tell" (an object moved, a timeline inconsistency, an alibi detail) in the episode's opening act, well before the detective notices it — the audience often spots it before Columbo does, which the show uses deliberately to build anticipation rather than surprise.
**Application:** |
  For a howcatchem structure, draft the culprit's "perfect plan" first, then identify the one specific, small flaw in it — plant that flaw visibly early, and structure the detective's investigation as a slow convergence on the exact same flaw the audience already half-noticed.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Character
**Name:** The detective's signature method as characterization
**Description:** Giving a detective a consistent, recognizable investigative style does double duty as both plot mechanism and character definition.
**Principle:** |
  A detective's method — what they notice, what they dismiss, how they talk through a case — is one of the most efficient tools available for characterization, because it's demonstrated through action across every case rather than told once. A consistent method also gives the reader a way to actively participate: once they know how this detective thinks, they can start applying the same lens to the clues themselves.
**Example:** |
  Holmes's method (physical trace evidence, near-total emotional detachment from a case) and Father Brown's method (psychological and moral insight from lived pastoral experience) aren't just different investigative techniques — they're direct expressions of who each character fundamentally is, making the detection itself a form of characterization rather than a separate layer bolted onto a personality.
**Application:** |
  Before drafting a case, be explicit (even just in your own notes) about what your detective's method actually is — what kind of evidence they trust, what kind they distrust, what question they always ask first — and then let that method visibly shape how they approach every scene, not just the reveal.
