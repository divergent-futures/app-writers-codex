## ENTRY
**Category:** The fair-play contract
**Name:** Clue visibility and the fair-play rule
**Description:** Every fact the detective uses in the final reveal must have been shown to the reader earlier, even if its significance wasn't clear at the time.
**Principle:** |
  The reader should be able to look back after the reveal and find every clue in the text — not necessarily understood, but present. A solution built on information withheld from the reader (an unmentioned twin, an undisclosed poison, a fact revealed only in the final chapter) breaks the genre's central promise and reads as a cheat, however clever it seems to the writer.
**Example:** |
  Ronald Knox's 1929 "Ten Commandments" and the "Detection Club" oath he helped formalize among Golden Age writers (including Christie and Sayers) were explicit attempts to codify this rule after readers began complaining about unfair mysteries — a genre self-regulating its own contract with its audience.
**Application:** |
  When drafting a reveal, work backward: list every fact the detective states in the denouement, then check each one appears on the page earlier. If a fact appears for the first time in the reveal itself, either plant it earlier or cut it from the solution.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Misdirection
**Name:** Constructing an honest red herring
**Description:** A false lead that misdirects the reader without lying to them — built from selective emphasis, not fabricated evidence.
**Principle:** |
  A red herring works by giving a suspect a real motive, a real opportunity, and a plausible-looking piece of evidence — then letting the reader's own assumptions do the misdirecting. The trick is emphasis, not deception: everything shown about the false suspect should be true, just incomplete or interpreted the way the reader naturally would.
**Example:** |
  Christie's method, most visible in Murder on the Orient Express, is to give nearly every character on the page a genuine, independently sufficient motive — so the red herrings aren't fake clues at all, just true ones that happen to point at the wrong (or, famously in that book, an insufficiently singular) conclusion.
**Application:** |
  Give your false suspect the same density of real, checkable detail you give your real culprit. A red herring built on a lie the narrative later has to retract feels like a cheat; a red herring built on true facts arranged to invite the wrong conclusion feels like fair play, even in hindsight.
