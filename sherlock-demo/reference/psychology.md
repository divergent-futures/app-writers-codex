## ENTRY
**Category:** Cognition & bias
**Name:** Eyewitness memory unreliability
**Description:** Human memory is reconstructive, not a recording — eyewitness testimony is far less reliable than it feels to the witness.
**Principle:** |
  Memory does not store events like video; it reconstructs them each time they're recalled, and each reconstruction is vulnerable to distortion from later information, suggestion, stress, and the passage of time. Confidence in a memory does not correlate reliably with its accuracy — a witness can be utterly certain and utterly wrong.
**Example:** |
  Elizabeth Loftus's research from the 1970s onward, including the famous "lost in the mall" studies, demonstrated that false memories can be implanted through suggestive questioning alone — work that has since reshaped how courts treat eyewitness testimony and contributed to numerous wrongful-conviction exonerations.
**Application:** |
  A detective who treats every witness account as provisional — cross-checking it against physical evidence rather than trusting it outright — reads as more realistically skilled than one who takes testimony at face value. Conflicting witness accounts are a legitimate, research-grounded source of red herrings, not just a convenient plot device.

**Category:** Reasoning & error
**Name:** Confirmation bias in investigation
**Description:** The tendency to notice and favor evidence that fits a theory already formed, and overlook what contradicts it.
**Principle:** |
  Once an investigator forms a working theory of a case, they tend to interpret ambiguous evidence as supporting that theory and to discount or explain away evidence that doesn't — not through dishonesty, but as a basic feature of how human reasoning works under uncertainty.
**Example:** |
  Confirmation bias is cited as a contributing factor in numerous real wrongful convictions, where investigators fixated on an early suspect and subsequently interpreted ambiguous forensic evidence in that suspect's favor while under-investigating alternative leads.
**Application:** |
  A genuinely brilliant fictional detective should be distinguished from a merely confident one partly by visible resistance to this bias — actively searching for evidence that would disprove their own leading theory, not just evidence that confirms it. This is a strong differentiator between a Holmes-type figure and a lesser, more ordinary investigator in the same story.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Interrogation & testimony
**Name:** False confessions
**Description:** Innocent people do, demonstrably and repeatedly, confess to crimes they did not commit — a real and well-documented phenomenon, not just a plot contrivance.
**Principle:** |
  False confessions occur through several documented mechanisms: prolonged, high-pressure interrogation exploiting exhaustion and stress; genuine confusion in vulnerable or suggestible individuals (particularly minors or those with certain cognitive or mental health conditions) about what they actually did; and a belief, however incorrect, that confessing will lead to a better outcome than continuing to deny involvement.
**Example:** |
  The Innocence Project has documented false confessions as a contributing factor in a substantial share of DNA-based exonerations in the United States — a genuine, measured phenomenon, not a rare fictional curiosity.
**Application:** |
  A detective who is suspicious of a "too clean" confession — inconsistent on small physical details a real culprit would know, or extracted after unusually long or aggressive questioning — is drawing on real, documented interrogation psychology, and this makes for a more sophisticated mystery than one where a confession is automatically treated as case-closing proof.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Profiling & method
**Name:** Criminal profiling's real validity and limits
**Description:** Behavioral profiling is a genuine investigative tool with real successes, but its predictive accuracy is more limited and contested than popular fiction usually portrays.
**Principle:** |
  Modern criminal profiling grew out of FBI Behavioral Science Unit work beginning in the 1970s, generating characteristics (likely age range, background, behavioral patterns) from crime-scene analysis. Academic reviews of profiling's actual predictive accuracy have been considerably more mixed than its popular reputation suggests — it functions better as one investigative input among several than as a reliably precise forecasting tool.
**Example:** |
  Real profiling successes (such as aspects of the Unabomber investigation) coexist with well-documented cases where profiles were vague enough to fit a wide range of suspects, or actively misdirected an investigation — a genuinely mixed track record worth reflecting honestly in fiction rather than treating profiling as infallible.
**Application:** |
  A detective character who treats a profile as a useful hypothesis to test against evidence — rather than a solved case in itself — reads as more credible and more interesting than one who profiles a culprit with impossible precision from minimal information.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Interrogation & testimony
**Name:** Deception cues and the limits of lie detection
**Description:** Popular belief in reliable behavioral "tells" for lying (avoiding eye contact, fidgeting) is considerably less scientifically supported than commonly assumed.
**Principle:** |
  Decades of psychological research have found that most commonly cited behavioral cues to deception (gaze aversion, nervous movement, etc.) show weak and inconsistent correlation with actual lying — people are, on average, not much better than chance at detecting lies from behavior alone, including trained professionals in many studies.
**Example:** |
  This research directly undercuts the reliability of the polygraph ("lie detector"), which measures physiological arousal rather than deception itself and is inadmissible as evidence in most U.S. courts for exactly this reason — genuine nervousness and genuine deception can produce identical physiological readings.
**Application:** |
  A detective whose method is "I can just tell when someone's lying" from body language alone is drawing on a real but scientifically shaky folk belief — using it as a starting hunch a detective then has to verify with actual evidence is more credible than treating it as a reliable standalone method.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Reasoning & error
**Name:** Apophenia — seeing patterns that aren't there
**Description:** The human tendency to perceive meaningful connections or patterns in random or unrelated data.
**Principle:** |
  Apophenia describes the mind's strong bias toward finding pattern and causality even in genuinely random information — useful in moderation (pattern recognition is core to good detective work) but a real liability when it produces false connections between coincidental, unrelated events.
**Example:** |
  The term was coined by psychiatrist Klaus Conrad in the 1950s studying the onset of psychosis, though the milder, everyday version of the phenomenon (seeing shapes in clouds, feeling a string of unrelated coincidences must be connected) is a normal feature of human cognition, not exclusively a symptom of illness.
**Application:** |
  A useful contrast between a brilliant detective and an unreliable or overconfident one: the brilliant detective's pattern-recognition is disciplined by testing it against evidence, while a flawed investigator (or a detective drawn deliberately as fallible) can be shown chasing a genuinely false pattern with total conviction — a realistic, psychologically grounded way to generate a wrong turn in an investigation.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Reasoning & error
**Name:** Occam's razor as a reasoning heuristic
**Description:** The principle that, among competing explanations, the one requiring the fewest assumptions is usually preferred — a genuine philosophical tool, not just a saying.
**Principle:** |
  Attributed to the 14th-century friar William of Ockham, the razor is a heuristic, not an infallible law: it favors simpler explanations as more likely to be correct, all else being equal, but doesn't guarantee the simplest explanation is true in any given case — reality is sometimes genuinely complicated.
**Example:** |
  Holmes's own dictum, "when you have eliminated the impossible, whatever remains, however improbable, must be the truth" (from The Sign of Four), is a related but distinct principle — it's about elimination of alternatives rather than preference for simplicity, and the two are sometimes conflated in popular discussion of the character's method.
**Application:** |
  A detective can use Occam's razor as a genuine reasoning tool while also being shown recognizing its limits — a satisfying mid-story misdirection can come from the detective (and reader) correctly favoring the simplest explanation, which then turns out to be wrong precisely because the real situation was genuinely, unusually complicated.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Group dynamics
**Name:** Groupthink among investigators
**Description:** A team of investigators can converge prematurely on a shared, incorrect theory, each member's doubts suppressed by the appearance of group consensus.
**Principle:** |
  Groupthink, a term coined by psychologist Irving Janis in the 1970s studying flawed U.S. policy decisions, describes how group pressure toward consensus and cohesion can suppress dissenting views and critical evaluation — a dynamic that applies as readily to a team of detectives converging on a suspect as to any other group decision-making process.
**Example:** |
  Documented failures in real criminal investigations have sometimes been attributed to exactly this dynamic — an investigative team converging early on a suspect, with junior officers' doubts going unvoiced or dismissed once senior figures had committed publicly to a theory.
**Application:** |
  A useful source of institutional friction for a procedural mystery: a detective who privately doubts the team's leading theory but faces real social and professional pressure to fall in line is a psychologically grounded, non-cartoonish way to generate internal conflict within an investigation, distinct from simple incompetence or corruption.
