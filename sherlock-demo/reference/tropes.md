## ENTRY
**Category:** Crime & method
**Name:** The locked-room mystery
**Description:** A crime committed inside a space that appears sealed from the inside, with no apparent way in or out for a culprit.
**Examples:**
  - **Work:** The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes ("The Speckled Band") — **Medium:** Short story (Arthur Conan Doyle) — **Year:** 1892 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Doyle sets up a bedroom sealed by a stuck bell-rope, a ventilator that shouldn't exist, and a bed bolted to the floor — every detail apparently decorative until Holmes reveals the room was built as a murder device, its "locked" nature explained by a trained snake entering through the false ventilator. The trope's core pleasure, the reader re-reading the crime scene and realizing every strange detail was a clue, is executed almost perfectly here.
  - **Work:** The Hollow Man (also published as The Three Coffins) — **Medium:** Novel (John Dickson Carr) — **Year:** 1935 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Carr devotes an entire chapter to Dr. Gideon Fell lecturing directly to the reader on the taxonomy of locked-room solutions — a genuinely useful primer for any writer attempting the form — before delivering a case with two impossible crimes and a snow-covered street with no footprints. It remains the genre's most self-aware and most cited locked-room novel.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Reveal & structure
**Name:** The drawing-room denouement
**Description:** The detective gathers every suspect in one room and walks the group through the solution live, unmasking the culprit in front of the other suspects.
**Examples:**
  - **Work:** The Murder of Roger Ackroyd — **Medium:** Novel (Agatha Christie) — **Year:** 1926 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Poirot's gathered-room reveal is the genre's most structurally audacious use of the device, because the detective is explicitly addressing the narrator as much as the suspects, and the narrator has been the culprit the entire time. The scene works because Christie plays scrupulously fair with the physical clues even while the narrator's account has quietly omitted the truth.
  - **Work:** Knives Out — **Medium:** Film (Rian Johnson) — **Year:** 2019 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Johnson stages a classic drawing-room gathering early in the film, then deliberately breaks the trope by having detective Benoit Blanc admit he doesn't yet know the answer — subverting audience expectation that this scene is always the reveal, and saving the real gathered-suspects unmasking for a redesigned final act that plays the convention straighter.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Reveal & structure
**Name:** The least-likely suspect
**Description:** The culprit is the character the reader (and often the other characters) trusted or dismissed most completely.
**Examples:**
  - **Work:** Murder on the Orient Express — **Medium:** Novel (Agatha Christie) — **Year:** 1934 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Christie inverts the trope entirely rather than simply executing it: instead of one unlikely culprit, every single suspect did it, which is only satisfying because each individually reads as too minor, too grief-stricken, or too incidental to the household to be "the" murderer. The least-likely-suspect payoff becomes least-likely-*solution* instead.
  - **Work:** Gone Girl — **Medium:** Novel (Gillian Flynn) — **Year:** 2012 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Not a classic detective novel, but a sharp modern use of the device: the missing wife, presumed victim, is revealed as the orchestrator of her own disappearance. The trope lands because the narrative structure (alternating diary and present-day chapters) has spent half the book actively building sympathy for her as the least-suspectable party.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Clues
**Name:** The dying clue or message
**Description:** A victim leaves a fragmentary message — spoken, written, or gestured — with their last moments of life, which the detective must decode.
**Examples:**
  - **Work:** A Study in Scarlet — **Medium:** Novel (Arthur Conan Doyle) — **Year:** 1887 — **Quality:** weak —
    **Text:** The word "RACHE" (German for "revenge") written in blood on the wall looks like a classic dying message but is in fact a deliberate red herring planted by the murderer to mislead police into thinking a woman named Rachel was involved — Doyle using the trope's familiar shape to subvert reader expectation rather than deliver a genuine clue.
  - **Work:** The Bishop Murder Case — **Medium:** Novel (S.S. Van Dine) — **Year:** 1929 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Van Dine builds an entire nursery-rhyme-themed murder spree around cryptic messages left near each body, each pointing toward the next victim — an influential Golden Age example of the dying/planted clue used as serial structure rather than a single-scene device.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Clues
**Name:** The unbreakable alibi
**Description:** A suspect appears to have airtight proof they could not have committed the crime, which the detective must find a way to crack.
**Examples:**
  - **Work:** Evil Under the Sun — **Medium:** Novel (Agatha Christie) — **Year:** 1941 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** The victim's husband appears to have been elsewhere at the exact time of death, corroborated by multiple witnesses — Poirot's solution hinges on proving the "time of death" itself was staged, attacking the alibi's foundation rather than the alibi's logic. A model for how to break an alibi without simply declaring a witness a liar.
  - **Work:** Columbo ("Any Old Port in a Storm") — **Medium:** TV episode — **Year:** 1973 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** As an inverted mystery, the audience already knows the killer built his alibi around a wine-tasting trip; the pleasure is watching Columbo methodically dismantle it piece by piece. Useful as a case study in alibi-breaking pacing, since the show has to sustain tension despite the audience already knowing the "who" and "how."

## ENTRY
**Category:** Character
**Name:** The loyal companion narrator
**Description:** A narrator of ordinary intelligence who accompanies the detective, records the case, and lets the reader experience genius as astonishment rather than having to inhabit it directly.
**Examples:**
  - **Work:** The complete Sherlock Holmes canon — **Medium:** Novels & short stories (Arthur Conan Doyle) — **Year:** 1887-1927 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Watson is the trope's foundational example and remains the standard every later companion-narrator is measured against — capable enough to be a credible witness, ordinary enough that the reader identifies with his bafflement rather than Holmes's certainty.
  - **Work:** The Hercule Poirot novels — **Medium:** Novels (Agatha Christie) — **Year:** 1920-1975 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Captain Hastings serves the identical structural function as Watson, explicitly and deliberately — Christie has acknowledged the debt directly — though Christie also wrote many Poirot novels with no fixed companion narrator at all, showing the device is a tool to reach for, not a structural requirement of the genre.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Method
**Name:** Disguise and misdirection
**Description:** A character (detective or criminal) adopts a false identity or appearance to gather information or evade detection.
**Examples:**
  - **Work:** A Scandal in Bohemia — **Medium:** Short story (Arthur Conan Doyle) — **Year:** 1891 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Holmes disguises himself as a drunken groom and later a clergyman to gather intelligence on Irene Adler's household — and is, famously, seen through by Adler herself, who disguises herself in turn as a young man to follow Holmes home undetected. The trope is used symmetrically, which is part of why the story reads as a genuine contest rather than a one-sided demonstration.
  - **Work:** The Talented Mr. Ripley — **Medium:** Novel (Patricia Highsmith) — **Year:** 1955 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Highsmith inverts the trope from detective-tool to criminal-engine: Tom Ripley's assumption of a murdered man's identity is the entire plot, not a single scene's misdirection. Useful for a writer wanting disguise to carry sustained narrative weight rather than a brief set piece.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Antagonist
**Name:** The criminal mastermind
**Description:** A single antagonist whose intelligence and reach rival or exceed the detective's own, operating a criminal enterprise the detective can barely see the edges of.
**Examples:**
  - **Work:** The Final Problem — **Medium:** Short story (Arthur Conan Doyle) — **Year:** 1893 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Professor Moriarty appears in exactly one story yet defines the shape every subsequent detective-fiction mastermind is compared against — deliberately given almost no interiority, functioning as pure intellectual threat rather than a rounded character, which is precisely what makes him legible as Holmes's equal without ever competing for reader sympathy.
  - **Work:** The Silence of the Lambs — **Medium:** Novel (Thomas Harris) — **Year:** 1988 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Hannibal Lecter modernizes the trope by giving the mastermind genuine charisma and psychological interiority rather than Moriarty's opacity — a useful contrast for a writer deciding whether their antagonist should remain a distant intellectual threat or become a magnetic on-page presence.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Structure
**Name:** The closed circle of suspects
**Description:** The setting physically limits who could have committed the crime to a small, known group — a country house, a train, an island, a snowed-in inn.
**Examples:**
  - **Work:** And Then There Were None — **Medium:** Novel (Agatha Christie) — **Year:** 1939 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Ten strangers isolated on an island, killed one by one according to a nursery rhyme, with no detective present at all — Christie pushes the closed circle to its logical extreme, since eventually the suspect pool and the victim pool become the same shrinking group. Widely considered the purest execution of the trope in the genre.
  - **Work:** Murder on the Orient Express — **Medium:** Novel (Agatha Christie) — **Year:** 1934 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** A train stalled by snowdrift gives Poirot a literal locked circle of suspects with no possibility of an outside culprit — the setting doing double duty as both atmosphere and plot mechanism, since the closed circle is what makes the eventual solution (everyone did it) even conceivable.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Character
**Name:** The wrongly accused
**Description:** An innocent character is suspected or formally charged, and the detective's real work is clearing them as much as finding the true culprit.
**Examples:**
  - **Work:** The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes ("The Boscombe Valley Mystery") — **Medium:** Short story (Arthur Conan Doyle) — **Year:** 1891 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** A son is arrested for his father's murder on strong circumstantial evidence; Holmes's investigation is driven as much by clearing an innocent man under pressure of an impending trial as by identifying the real killer, giving the case genuine time pressure beyond simple curiosity.
  - **Work:** The Fugitive — **Medium:** Film (Andrew Davis) — **Year:** 1993 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Uses the wrongly-accused trope from the accused's own point of view rather than the detective's, with Dr. Richard Kimble forced to become his own investigator while evading Deputy Marshal Gerard — a useful model for structuring a mystery where the protagonist's stakes are personal survival, not professional curiosity.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Clues
**Name:** The cipher or coded message
**Description:** A message written in a private or invented code that the detective must crack to advance the case.
**Examples:**
  - **Work:** The Adventure of the Dancing Men — **Medium:** Short story (Arthur Conan Doyle) — **Year:** 1903 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Holmes cracks a substitution cipher disguised as a child's stick-figure drawing using frequency analysis of the English language — one of the most technically accurate cryptography sequences in the classic canon, and a direct model for how a fair-play cipher clue should be constructed (the reader can, with effort, actually decode it too).
  - **Work:** National Treasure — **Medium:** Film (Jon Turteltosh) — **Year:** 2004 — **Quality:** weak —
    **Text:** The film's ciphers and hidden maps prioritize spectacle over solvability — the audience is shown a code exists but is never given enough information to crack it themselves, breaking the fair-play version of the trope even though it remains entertaining as pure adventure.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Character
**Name:** The gentleman thief
**Description:** A charming, highly skilled criminal who steals with style and a personal code rather than violence, often as much antihero as antagonist.
**Examples:**
  - **Work:** Arsène Lupin, Gentleman-Burglar — **Medium:** Short story collection (Maurice Leblanc) — **Year:** 1907 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Leblanc created Lupin explicitly as a French counter to the very English Sherlock Holmes (an early version even directly crossed over with a thinly-disguised "Herlock Sholmès" before legal pressure forced a rename) — the gentleman thief as a mirror-image response to the great detective, criminal ingenuity mapped onto the same deductive-showmanship structure.
  - **Work:** To Catch a Thief — **Medium:** Film (Alfred Hitchcock) — **Year:** 1955 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** A reformed jewel thief must catch a copycat to clear his own name — the trope used to generate a detective plot from the criminal's side of the ledger, with the thief's specialist knowledge of the "trade" functioning exactly like a detective's specialist knowledge of deduction.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Structure
**Name:** The copycat crime
**Description:** A new crime is deliberately staged to resemble an earlier one — a real case, a famous unsolved crime, or even a work of fiction — misdirecting investigators toward the wrong pattern.
**Examples:**
  - **Work:** The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes ("The Adventure of the Blue Carbuncle") — **Medium:** Short story (Arthur Conan Doyle) — **Year:** 1892 — **Quality:** weak —
    **Text:** Not a true copycat case, but included as a useful negative example: the story's Christmas goose theft is mistaken for a pattern of jewel theft, showing how easily investigators (and readers) can be misled by assuming two similar-looking incidents share a cause — the seed of the copycat trope without the trope's full deliberate staging.
  - **Work:** Seven — **Medium:** Film (David Fincher) — **Year:** 1995 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** The killer stages each murder to match one of the seven deadly sins in sequence, effectively making his own crime spree a copycat of a moral framework rather than a specific prior case — detectives Mills and Somerset must recognize and anticipate the pattern before the final sin is completed, giving the copycat structure real forward tension rather than only retrospective analysis.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Structure
**Name:** The inverted mystery (howcatchem)
**Description:** The audience sees the crime and the culprit from the start; the tension comes from watching the detective figure out what the audience already knows.
**Examples:**
  - **Work:** Columbo — **Medium:** TV series — **Year:** 1968-2003 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** The defining example of the form: nearly every episode opens by showing the murder and murderer in full, then spends the runtime on Lieutenant Columbo's patient, deceptively bumbling accumulation of the one detail that breaks the killer's plan. Proof that a "whodunit" question isn't required for a mystery to sustain tension.
  - **Work:** Crime and Punishment — **Medium:** Novel (Fyodor Dostoevsky) — **Year:** 1866 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Predates the genre's formal conventions but is structurally a howcatchem: the reader watches Raskolnikov commit murder in the opening chapters, and the novel's tension comes entirely from psychological unraveling and the investigator Porfiry's slow, almost conversational pursuit — a model for the form's literary, interiority-driven end of the spectrum.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Structure
**Name:** The cold case reopened
**Description:** A detective revisits a crime long considered solved, unsolvable, or forgotten, and finds it was wrong all along.
**Examples:**
  - **Work:** The Daughter of Time — **Medium:** Novel (Josephine Tey) — **Year:** 1951 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** A detective recovering from injury investigates the five-hundred-year-old case of Richard III and the Princes in the Tower entirely from a hospital bed, using only historical documents — arguably the purest possible cold-case structure, since the case is so old that no living witness or physical evidence remains at all, only the accumulated record.
  - **Work:** True Detective (Season 1) — **Medium:** TV series — **Year:** 2014 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Uses a reopened cold case as a frame for dual timelines — detectives interviewed years after their original investigation are forced to reconstruct and reconsider what they missed the first time — a structure that lets the show dramatize both the original case and the psychological toll of having gotten it wrong.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Antagonist
**Name:** The rival detective
**Description:** A second investigator — official or amateur — competes with or opposes the protagonist's methods, providing a contrasting model of how to solve the case.
**Examples:**
  - **Work:** The complete Sherlock Holmes canon — **Medium:** Novels & short stories (Arthur Conan Doyle) — **Year:** 1887-1927 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Inspector Lestrade functions as a sustained rival-detective figure across the whole canon — never incompetent, but consistently outpaced, his orthodox procedural method serving as the baseline the reader measures Holmes's brilliance against. The relationship's mutual respect, grudging on Lestrade's side, keeps the rivalry from curdling into simple mockery.
  - **Work:** A Study in Scarlet — **Medium:** Novel (Arthur Conan Doyle) — **Year:** 1887 — **Quality:** strong —
    **Text:** Doyle actually deploys two rival detectives at once, Lestrade and Gregson, explicitly written as professionally competitive with each other as well as with Holmes — a useful structural note that a rival doesn't have to be singular, and inter-rival friction can generate its own scenes independent of the protagonist.
