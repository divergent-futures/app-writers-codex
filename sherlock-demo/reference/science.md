**Category:** Forensic science fundamentals
**Name:** Locard's exchange principle
**Description:** Every contact leaves a trace — the foundational assumption underneath all forensic detective work.
**Principle:** |
  Formulated by French criminologist Edmond Locard in the early 20th century: "every contact leaves a trace." When two objects or people come into contact, material is exchanged between them — fibers, hair, skin cells, soil, residue — and that exchange, however microscopic, is in principle detectable. It is the single organizing assumption underneath fingerprint work, trace fiber analysis, and modern DNA forensics alike.
**Example:** |
  Locard developed the principle while running one of the first forensic laboratories, in Lyon, France, and used it to solve cases via microscopic analysis of dust and fibers found under suspects' fingernails and on their clothing — a discipline barely imagined in Holmes's own era but a direct descendant of Holmes's fictional method of scrutinizing overlooked physical detail.
**Application:** |
  A detective character built on this principle should treat every scene as a two-way exchange: the criminal leaves something behind, and the criminal carries something away. A satisfying fair-play mystery can be built by planting one clue of each kind — something left at the scene, and something the culprit is later found still carrying.

**Name:** Forensic entomology and time of death
**Description:** Using the life cycle of insects found on a body to estimate how long it has been dead.
**Principle:** |
  Blowflies typically arrive at a body within minutes of death and lay eggs; the eggs progress through predictable larval stages at rates that depend on temperature and species. By identifying which insect life stages are present, an examiner can estimate a fairly precise window since death, often tighter than temperature-based estimates alone can provide.
**Example:** |
  The method dates to 13th-century China (a magistrate's manual, the Xi Yuan Ji Lu, describes using fly activity to identify a murder weapon by the blood residue flies were drawn to) and was formalized as modern forensic entomology in the late 19th and 20th centuries — meaning a Holmes-era detective could plausibly know of it as an emerging or half-formed technique, not yet standard practice.
**Application:** |
  Useful for any case where the timeline hinges on exactly when a body was left somewhere versus when it was found — a detective citing insect stages to contradict a suspect's alibi is a period-plausible, scientifically real deduction rather than a hand-wave.

**Name:** Fingerprint formation and uniqueness
**Description:** Why every person's fingerprints are believed to be unique, and how ridge patterns actually form.
**Principle:** |
  Friction ridge patterns form in the womb from a combination of genetic instruction and essentially random pressure and positioning as the fetus develops — meaning even identical twins, who share genetics, do not share fingerprints. The patterns are stable for life barring injury deep enough to scar the dermis, not just the outer skin layer.
**Example:** |
  Sir Francis Galton's 1892 book Finger Prints established the statistical basis for treating prints as effectively unique, estimating the odds of two people sharing a full print at roughly 1 in 64 billion — work that directly enabled the Galton-Henry classification system Scotland Yard adopted in 1901.
**Application:** |
  A writer can use the "identical twins, different fingerprints" fact directly as a plot device — a twin-swap mystery that seems to defeat every other identification method (voice, appearance, even some behavioral tells) can still be cracked by prints, giving a detective a clean, scientifically real way to resolve what looks like an impossible identity puzzle.

**Category:** Forensic science fundamentals
**Name:** Ballistics and rifling
**Description:** How the internal grooves of a gun barrel mark a fired bullet in a way that can be matched back to that specific weapon.
**Principle:** |
  A gun barrel's rifling — spiral grooves cut into the interior to spin the bullet for stability — leaves microscopic scratches unique to that individual barrel, shaped by manufacturing tool wear and use over time. Comparing the marks on a recovered bullet against a test-fired round from a suspect weapon can, in principle, identify or exclude that weapon as the source.
**Example:** |
  Calvin Goddard's development of the comparison microscope in the 1920s, allowing two bullets to be viewed side by side under matched magnification, is generally credited with making ballistic matching a rigorous, court-usable technique rather than a rough visual estimate — notably, this places reliable ballistic matching after the Holmes canon's own setting.
**Application:** |
  For a period-accurate Victorian-era case, ballistic matching should be treated as impossible or extremely crude — a detective in this era can identify a bullet's caliber and rough weapon type, but individualized "this exact gun fired this exact bullet" matching is genuinely anachronistic before the 1920s.

**Category:** Forensic science fundamentals
**Name:** Bloodstain pattern analysis
**Description:** Reconstructing the mechanics of a violent act — angle of impact, sequence of blows, position of victim and attacker — from the shape and distribution of blood droplets at a scene.
**Principle:** |
  A blood droplet's shape on a surface encodes information about the angle and velocity at which it traveled: a droplet that hits a surface straight on is round, while one traveling at a shallow angle stretches into an elongated teardrop pointing in the direction of travel. Multiple droplets can be traced back geometrically to a common point of origin.
**Example:** |
  The discipline was formalized in the mid-20th century and became widely known to the public through high-profile criminal trials in which bloodstain pattern experts testified about the geometry of an attack — though courts and scientific bodies have since raised real concerns about the technique's subjectivity and error rate when practiced by insufficiently trained analysts.
**Application:** |
  Useful for a scene where the physical arrangement of evidence needs to contradict a suspect's account — "your story has you standing here, but the spatter pattern says the blow came from over there" is a scientifically grounded way to break an alibi through physical reconstruction rather than testimony alone.

**Name:** Toxicology and dose-response
**Description:** The basic principle that a substance's effect depends on quantity, not just presence — "the dose makes the poison."
**Principle:** |
  Nearly any substance can be harmful at a high enough dose, and many substances considered poisons are harmless or even therapeutic at low doses — arsenic, digitalis, and opium all have or had legitimate medical uses. Toxicology as a science is fundamentally about establishing dose-response relationships, not simply identifying "safe" versus "dangerous" substances.
**Example:** |
  This principle is often summarized by the 16th-century physician Paracelsus's dictum "the dose makes the poison" — a genuinely old scientific idea, meaning a Victorian-era physician character could plausibly articulate it even though modern toxicology's precise measurement tools didn't yet exist.
**Application:** |
  A fair-play poisoning mystery can hinge on this principle directly: a substance found in a victim's system isn't damning by itself if it's also present in something the household uses medicinally in small amounts — the detective's job becomes proving the dose, not just the presence.

**Category:** Forensic science fundamentals
**Name:** Decomposition and time-of-death estimation
**Description:** Using a body's physical changes after death — body temperature, muscle stiffening, blood pooling — to estimate when death occurred.
**Principle:** |
  Three classic indicators, each useful over a different time window: algor mortis (body cooling toward ambient temperature, useful for the first roughly 24 hours), rigor mortis (temporary muscle stiffening that sets in over several hours and resolves after roughly 24-48 hours), and livor mortis (blood pooling by gravity, which fixes in place after several hours and can reveal if a body was moved after death).
**Example:** |
  A body found with livor mortis fixed in a position inconsistent with how it was discovered is real, physically grounded evidence that it was moved post-mortem — a technique any period detective could plausibly use, since basic observation of these three signs predates modern instrumentation by centuries.
**Application:** |
  "The body was moved after death" is one of the most useful, fair-play-compatible reveals available to a mystery writer, because livor mortis makes it independently verifiable rather than a detective's unsupported assertion — the reader can, in principle, be shown the same clue.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Modern forensic science
**Name:** DNA fingerprinting fundamentals
**Description:** How genetic material can identify an individual with near-certainty, and why it revolutionized forensic science.
**Principle:** |
  Certain regions of human DNA vary enormously between individuals even though the vast majority of the genome is near-identical across humans. By comparing a set of these highly variable regions between a crime-scene sample and a suspect, forensic scientists can calculate odds of a match so large (often in the billions to one) that it functions as practical certainty.
**Example:** |
  Sir Alec Jeffreys developed DNA fingerprinting in 1984 at the University of Leicester, and it was first used forensically in 1986 to both convict a real culprit and exonerate an initial suspect in a British murder case — one of the technique's earliest uses was clearing an innocent man, not just convicting a guilty one.
**Application:** |
  Any mystery set after the mid-1980s has to reckon with DNA evidence's existence — either the plot accounts for why DNA testing wasn't available, wasn't decisive, or was deliberately avoided by the culprit, or the mystery risks feeling like it's ignoring an obvious investigative tool.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Modern forensic science
**Name:** Digital forensics: metadata and hashing
**Description:** How digital files carry hidden information beyond their visible content, and how investigators verify a file hasn't been altered.
**Principle:** |
  Nearly every digital file carries metadata — creation date, device of origin, GPS coordinates, edit history — often invisible in normal use but recoverable with the right tools. Separately, cryptographic hashing (converting a file into a fixed-length code that changes completely if even one bit of the file changes) lets investigators prove a piece of digital evidence hasn't been tampered with since collection.
**Example:** |
  Photo metadata (EXIF data) has repeatedly undone real-world criminals who posted images without realizing embedded GPS coordinates or timestamps contradicted their stated alibi — a genuinely common real investigative technique, not just a fictional convenience.
**Application:** |
  A modern-set mystery can use a culprit's carelessness with metadata as a clean, technically accurate "gotcha" clue — a photo posted to establish an alibi that actually proves the opposite once its hidden data is examined.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Forensic science fundamentals
**Name:** Forensic document examination
**Description:** Determining whether a document (a letter, a will, a signature) is genuine, forged, or altered.
**Principle:** |
  Document examiners analyze handwriting consistency, ink and paper age, pressure patterns, and — for typed or printed documents — mechanical characteristics of the device that produced them. The discipline sits between hard science and trained pattern-recognition expertise, and its reliability varies considerably by sub-technique; some methods (like ink dating) are more scientifically rigorous than others (like general handwriting "style" comparison, which courts increasingly treat with more caution than in the past).
**Example:** |
  Real forgery cases have turned on details as small as anachronistic paper composition (a document supposedly decades old written on paper stock not manufactured until years later) — physical material analysis can sometimes settle a case more decisively than the handwriting itself.
**Application:** |
  A forged will or letter is a classic mystery-plot object; grounding its detection in something physically checkable (paper stock, ink chemistry, a typewriter's individual mechanical quirks, per Holmes's own "A Case of Identity") reads as more satisfying and fair-play than a detective simply asserting the handwriting "looks wrong."
