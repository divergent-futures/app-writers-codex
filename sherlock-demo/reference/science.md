## ENTRY
**Category:** Forensic science fundamentals
**Name:** Locard's exchange principle
**Description:** Every contact leaves a trace — the foundational assumption underneath all forensic detective work.
**Principle:** |
  Formulated by French criminologist Edmond Locard in the early 20th century: "every contact leaves a trace." When two objects or people come into contact, material is exchanged between them — fibers, hair, skin cells, soil, residue — and that exchange, however microscopic, is in principle detectable. It is the single organizing assumption underneath fingerprint work, trace fiber analysis, and modern DNA forensics alike.
**Example:** |
  Locard developed the principle while running one of the first forensic laboratories, in Lyon, France, and used it to solve cases via microscopic analysis of dust and fibers found under suspects' fingernails and on their clothing — a discipline barely imagined in Holmes's own era but a direct descendant of Holmes's fictional method of scrutinizing overlooked physical detail.
**Application:** |
  A detective character built on this principle should treat every scene as a two-way exchange: the criminal leaves something behind, and the criminal carries something away. A satisfying fair-play mystery can be built by planting one clue of each kind — something left at the scene, and something the culprit is later found still carrying.

## ENTRY
**Category:** Forensic science fundamentals
**Name:** Forensic entomology and time of death
**Description:** Using the life cycle of insects found on a body to estimate how long it has been dead.
**Principle:** |
  Blowflies typically arrive at a body within minutes of death and lay eggs; the eggs progress through predictable larval stages at rates that depend on temperature and species. By identifying which insect life stages are present, an examiner can estimate a fairly precise window since death, often tighter than temperature-based estimates alone can provide.
**Example:** |
  The method dates to 13th-century China (a magistrate's manual, the Xi Yuan Ji Lu, describes using fly activity to identify a murder weapon by the blood residue flies were drawn to) and was formalized as modern forensic entomology in the late 19th and 20th centuries — meaning a Holmes-era detective could plausibly know of it as an emerging or half-formed technique, not yet standard practice.
**Application:** |
  Useful for any case where the timeline hinges on exactly when a body was left somewhere versus when it was found — a detective citing insect stages to contradict a suspect's alibi is a period-plausible, scientifically real deduction rather than a hand-wave.
