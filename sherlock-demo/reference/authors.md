## ENTRY
**Category:** Foundational
**Name:** Arthur Conan Doyle
**Description:** Creator of Sherlock Holmes; the writer who turned Poe's invention into the genre's most durable franchise.
**Meta:** 1859-1930 · British
**Known For:** |
  A Scottish physician who began writing Holmes stories to supplement a struggling medical practice, Doyle produced four novels and fifty-six short stories about the detective over four decades, along with historical fiction, science fiction (the Professor Challenger stories), and — notoriously — earnest advocacy for spiritualism late in life. He tried repeatedly to kill Holmes off and retire the character; public demand brought him back each time.
**Signature:** |
  The Holmes stories run on the deduction-as-showmanship set piece (reading a stranger's history from physical detail before they've spoken) and the Watson narrator device. Doyle's prose is brisk, plot-forward, and unafraid of melodrama; the short-story case-of-the-week format, serialized in The Strand Magazine, shaped how popular fiction would be published for the next century.
**Works:**
  - **Title:** A Study in Scarlet — **Year:** 1887 — **Note:** Holmes and Watson's first case; introduces the pair — **Start:** true
  - **Title:** The Sign of Four — **Year:** 1890 — **Note:** the Agra treasure, and Watson's courtship of Mary Morstan — **Start:** false
  - **Title:** The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes — **Year:** 1892 — **Note:** the first and most famous short-story collection — **Start:** false
  - **Title:** The Hound of the Baskervilles — **Year:** 1902 — **Note:** the best-known novel; gothic atmosphere on Dartmoor — **Start:** false
  - **Title:** His Last Bow — **Year:** 1917 — **Note:** wartime-era collection, Holmes drawn out of retirement — **Start:** false

## ENTRY
**Category:** Genre-split
**Name:** Agatha Christie
**Description:** The best-selling novelist of all time; formalized the fair-play puzzle-box mystery.
**Meta:** 1890-1976 · British
**Known For:** |
  Created Hercule Poirot and Miss Marple across more than sixty novels, and is credited with perfecting the "fair play" contract between author and reader — every clue the detective uses to solve the case is shown to the reader in plain sight before the reveal. Also wrote the West End's longest-running play, The Mousetrap.
**Signature:** |
  Closed-circle settings (a country house, a train, an island) that keep the suspect pool small and knowable; the honest red herring, built from selective emphasis rather than any lie to the reader; and a gathered-room reveal that walks back through the clue trail in front of the assembled suspects.
**Works:**
  - **Title:** The Murder of Roger Ackroyd — **Year:** 1926 — **Note:** the novel that broke the genre's rules about narrator trust — **Start:** true
  - **Title:** Murder on the Orient Express — **Year:** 1934 — **Note:** twelve suspects, one confined train car — **Start:** false
  - **Title:** And Then There Were None — **Year:** 1939 — **Note:** ten strangers, an island, no detective at all — **Start:** false

## ENTRY
**Category:** Foundational
**Name:** Edgar Allan Poe
**Description:** Widely credited as the genre's inventor, via three short stories starring C. Auguste Dupin in the 1840s.
**Meta:** 1809-1849 · American
**Known For:** |
  Better known in his own time and after for gothic horror and poetry ("The Raven"), Poe nonetheless invented essentially the entire detective-fiction grammar in just three stories: the eccentric genius detective, the less-brilliant narrator-companion, the solve-then-explain reveal structure, and the "impossible" crime with a rigorously logical solution.
**Signature:** |
  Dense, formal, essayistic prose by modern standards — the pleasure is intellectual rather than atmospheric or voice-driven. Poe is read today for structural invention more than for style to directly imitate.
**Works:**
  - **Title:** The Murders in the Rue Morgue — **Year:** 1841 — **Note:** the first Dupin story; widely called the first detective story — **Start:** true
  - **Title:** The Mystery of Marie Rogêt — **Year:** 1842 — **Note:** an armchair-detective analysis of a real unsolved murder, thinly fictionalized — **Start:** false
  - **Title:** The Purloined Letter — **Year:** 1844 — **Note:** a "hidden in plain sight" puzzle, hugely influential on later psychological-misdirection plots — **Start:** false

## ENTRY
**Category:** Foundational
**Name:** Wilkie Collins
**Description:** Author of what's often called the first true detective novel; pioneered the multi-narrator mystery structure.
**Meta:** 1824-1889 · British
**Known For:** |
  A close friend and sometime collaborator of Charles Dickens, Collins wrote sensation novels that blended mystery, gothic atmosphere, and social critique — his sympathetic, non-judgmental treatment of opium addiction and mental illness was unusually progressive for the period.
**Signature:** |
  Multiple narrators relaying the same events from different, partial, sometimes unreliable perspectives, with the truth assembled by the reader from the gaps between accounts — a structural innovation that predates and influenced the entire genre's later experiments with narrative reliability.
**Works:**
  - **Title:** The Woman in White — **Year:** 1860 — **Note:** sensation-novel landmark; multiple narrators, a woman's stolen identity — **Start:** true
  - **Title:** The Moonstone — **Year:** 1868 — **Note:** often called the first true detective novel; Sergeant Cuff — **Start:** false

## ENTRY
**Category:** Genre-split
**Name:** Raymond Chandler
**Description:** Defined the hardboiled private-eye voice; wrote the genre's own manifesto against the Golden Age puzzle box.
**Meta:** 1888-1959 · American
**Known For:** |
  Began writing crime fiction in his forties after losing an oil-company executive job during the Depression, publishing in pulp magazines (Black Mask) before his novels. Created private investigator Philip Marlowe, a morally weary man navigating a corrupt Los Angeles.
**Signature:** |
  Voice-driven first-person narration where simile and metaphor carry as much weight as plot; famously loose plotting in service of atmosphere and moral tone over airtight puzzle logic. His 1944 essay "The Simple Art of Murder" explicitly argued crime fiction should depict "a real world," not a bloodless puzzle.
**Works:**
  - **Title:** The Big Sleep — **Year:** 1939 — **Note:** Marlowe's debut; blackmail, pornography, a dying oil tycoon's family — **Start:** true
  - **Title:** The Long Goodbye — **Year:** 1953 — **Note:** widely considered Chandler's most emotionally mature novel — **Start:** false

## ENTRY
**Category:** Genre-split
**Name:** Dorothy L. Sayers
**Description:** Gave the classic detective genuine long-arc character development — falling in love, doubting himself, changing across a series.
**Meta:** 1893-1957 · British
**Known For:** |
  One of the first women to earn an Oxford degree, Sayers brought real literary ambition to genre fiction; her Lord Peter Wimsey novels grew steadily more serious and psychologically complex across the series, particularly once his eventual wife, detective novelist Harriet Vane, entered the books.
**Signature:** |
  A detective allowed to change — Wimsey is not the same static figure across sixty stories the way Holmes is across the whole canon. Prose with genuine literary ambition sitting comfortably inside fair-play puzzle-plotting; deep, often technical research (bell-ringing, advertising, academia) woven into each novel's setting.
**Works:**
  - **Title:** Strong Poison — **Year:** 1930 — **Note:** introduces Harriet Vane, on trial for murder — **Start:** true
  - **Title:** The Nine Tailors — **Year:** 1934 — **Note:** a Fenland churchyard body and English bell-ringing as murder method — **Start:** false
  - **Title:** Gaudy Night — **Year:** 1935 — **Note:** widely considered her masterpiece; almost no violence, all intellectual tension — **Start:** false

## ENTRY
**Category:** Genre-split
**Name:** G.K. Chesterton
**Description:** Created Father Brown, a detective who reasons from psychological and moral insight rather than physical-clue deduction.
**Meta:** 1874-1936 · British
**Known For:** |
  A prolific essayist, novelist, and Christian apologist as well as a mystery writer, Chesterton built Father Brown around his own experience of the Catholic confessional — the character solves crimes by understanding what kind of person could or couldn't commit a given act, not by reading physical trace evidence.
**Signature:** |
  Paradox and moral inversion — Chesterton's stories often hinge on a perceptual trick (the culprit was overlooked because they seemed to belong, like a postman no one really looks at) rather than a forensic clue chain. A genuinely different detective archetype from Holmes's "reasoning machine."
**Works:**
  - **Title:** The Innocence of Father Brown — **Year:** 1911 — **Note:** the first Father Brown collection — **Start:** true
  - **Title:** The Wisdom of Father Brown — **Year:** 1914 — **Note:** the second collection, deepening the character — **Start:** false

## ENTRY
**Category:** Genre-split
**Name:** Dashiell Hammett
**Description:** A former Pinkerton detective who founded the hardboiled school before Chandler, writing crime fiction from lived investigative experience.
**Meta:** 1894-1961 · American
**Known For:** |
  Worked as a real Pinkerton operative before turning to fiction, giving his crime writing a procedural authenticity Golden Age puzzle-box writers rarely matched. Created Sam Spade and, separately, the influential husband-and-wife detective duo Nick and Nora Charles.
**Signature:** |
  Terse, unadorned prose — "objective" narration that describes action and dialogue without editorializing on a character's internal state, letting the reader infer motive and feeling from behavior alone. A direct influence on Chandler and on hardboiled fiction generally.
**Works:**
  - **Title:** Red Harvest — **Year:** 1929 — **Note:** the Continental Op cleans up a thoroughly corrupt town — **Start:** true
  - **Title:** The Maltese Falcon — **Year:** 1930 — **Note:** Sam Spade; arguably the hardboiled genre's most famous single novel — **Start:** false

## ENTRY
**Category:** Genre-split
**Name:** John Dickson Carr
**Description:** The genre's foremost specialist in locked-room and impossible-crime puzzles.
**Meta:** 1906-1977 · American (long resident in England)
**Known For:** |
  Wrote dozens of novels devoted almost entirely to "impossible crime" puzzles — murders committed in sealed rooms, footprints that stop in open snow, disappearances with no possible exit — and is still considered the form's master. Wrote as both John Dickson Carr and under the pen name Carter Dickson.
**Signature:** |
  Meticulous, fair-play-obsessed puzzle construction; his character Dr. Gideon Fell at one point delivers a direct lecture to the reader cataloguing the taxonomy of locked-room solutions, functioning as both story and craft manual simultaneously.
**Works:**
  - **Title:** The Hollow Man (The Three Coffins) — **Year:** 1935 — **Note:** widely voted the greatest locked-room mystery ever written — **Start:** true

## ENTRY
**Category:** Adjacent / procedural lineage
**Name:** Georges Simenon
**Description:** Extraordinarily prolific Belgian writer whose Commissaire Maigret novels shaped the European police-procedural tradition.
**Meta:** 1903-1989 · Belgian
**Known For:** |
  Wrote roughly 75 Maigret novels (plus hundreds of other novels under his own name and pseudonyms), often completing a full manuscript in a matter of days. Maigret solves cases through patient, empathetic immersion in a suspect's world and psychology rather than flashy deduction.
**Signature:** |
  Spare, atmospheric prose focused on place and mood — Paris streets, weather, small daily rituals — over intricate plotting. Maigret's method is closer to a detective absorbing a milieu than solving a puzzle, a quieter, more procedural cousin to both the Golden Age and hardboiled traditions.
**Works:**
  - **Title:** Pietr the Latvian — **Year:** 1931 — **Note:** the first Maigret novel — **Start:** true

## ENTRY
**Category:** Genre-split
**Name:** Ellery Queen
**Description:** Pen name (and detective-character name) shared by cousins Frederic Dannay and Manfred Lee; wrote intricate, hyper-fair-play American puzzle mysteries.
**Meta:** Dannay 1905-1982, Lee 1905-1971 · American
**Known For:** |
  Wrote dozens of novels under the joint pseudonym, all featuring a detective also named Ellery Queen — an unusual, deliberate blurring of author and character. Many early novels include a formal "Challenge to the Reader" printed directly in the text, stating that every clue needed to solve the case has now been presented.
**Signature:** |
  Extremely rigorous, almost mathematical puzzle construction — arguably the most literal execution of the fair-play contract in the genre's history, given the explicit reader challenge built into the books themselves.
**Works:**
  - **Title:** The Roman Hat Mystery — **Year:** 1929 — **Note:** the first Ellery Queen novel — **Start:** true

## ENTRY
**Category:** Adjacent / modern
**Name:** P.D. James
**Description:** Brought literary depth and procedural realism to the British detective novel in the second half of the 20th century.
**Meta:** 1920-2014 · British
**Known For:** |
  Worked for years in the National Health Service and the Home Office (including in the criminal and police departments) before her writing career took off, giving her procedural detail real institutional grounding. Created poet-detective Adam Dalgliesh.
**Signature:** |
  Slow-burn, psychologically dense plotting closer to literary fiction than puzzle-box mystery — often called a direct heir to Sayers's ambition to give the detective genuine interiority, updated with a modern procedural's institutional realism.
**Works:**
  - **Title:** Cover Her Face — **Year:** 1962 — **Note:** the first Adam Dalgliesh novel — **Start:** true

## ENTRY
**Category:** Adjacent / modern
**Name:** Tana French
**Description:** Modern Irish crime novelist whose Dublin Murder Squad series updates the police procedural with literary-grade prose and deep character interiority.
**Meta:** born 1973 · Irish-American
**Known For:** |
  Trained as an actor before turning to fiction; each Dublin Murder Squad novel shifts to a different detective as protagonist, letting the series explore how the same institution and city look different depending on whose psychology is narrating.
**Signature:** |
  Forensic and procedural realism balanced against sustained, often unreliable first-person interiority — closer to Sayers's project of giving a detective a real inner life than to Christie's puzzle-box tradition, but grounded in a fully modern procedural setting.
**Works:**
  - **Title:** In the Woods — **Year:** 2007 — **Note:** the first Dublin Murder Squad novel — **Start:** true
