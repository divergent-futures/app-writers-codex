## READING
**Title:** The Murders in the Rue Morgue, The Purloined Letter, The Mystery of Marie Rogêt
**Author:** Edgar Allan Poe
**Status:** read
**Gives:** The genre's origin grammar — the eccentric genius detective, the less-brilliant narrator-companion, and the solve-then-explain reveal structure, invented in three short stories in the 1840s.
**Tags:** foundation, detective-canon, reveal-structure
**Correlation:** Poe shows the solution first, then rewinds to show how the detective got there, clue by clue — still the dominant reveal shape in the genre today. Read for structure, not for prose style; his sentences are dense and formal by modern standards.
**Takeaways:**
  - Borrow the solve-then-explain reveal shape
  - Study the narrator-companion relationship in embryo, before Doyle perfected it

## READING
**Title:** The Complete Sherlock Holmes canon
**Author:** Arthur Conan Doyle
**Status:** read
**Gives:** The Holmes/Watson dynamic — the genre's most influential relationship — plus the case-of-the-week structure and deduction-as-showmanship set pieces.
**Tags:** foundation, detective-canon, narrator-device
**Correlation:** A narrator-companion of ordinary intelligence is almost always a better viewpoint character than the detective. The reader feels smart watching Watson be astonished, even while staying behind Holmes. Give your detective a signature method and let the reader learn to anticipate it, the way they'd learn a magician's style.
**Takeaways:**
  - Borrow the Watson device — a companion narrator of ordinary intelligence
  - Give the detective a signature method as recurring showmanship
  - Watch for fair-play looseness — Holmes sometimes knows things the reader had no way to deduce; decide early whether your detective plays strictly fair or not

## READING
**Title:** The Moonstone; The Woman in White
**Author:** Wilkie Collins
**Status:** read
**Gives:** Often called the first true detective novel. Multiple narrators telling the same events from different angles, the truth assembled by the reader from the gaps between accounts.
**Tags:** foundation, detective-canon, multi-narrator
**Correlation:** The multi-narrator structure is a powerful tool if your book has more than one investigator, or if you want the reader to distrust any single account — it solves the problem of a mystery too large for one Watson to witness firsthand.
**Takeaways:**
  - Borrow the multi-narrator, partial-account structure for mysteries too big for one witness
  - Watch for Victorian pacing — patient in a way that reads slow to modern readers; study the structure more than the sentence rhythm

## READING
**Title:** The Murder of Roger Ackroyd; And Then There Were None; Murder on the Orient Express
**Author:** Agatha Christie
**Status:** read
**Gives:** The fair-play contract formalized — every clue the detective uses is shown to the reader in plain sight before the reveal. The closed-circle suspect pool. The honest red herring.
**Tags:** genre-split, fair-play, puzzle-structure
**Correlation:** If a detective story should feel like a puzzle the reader can play along with, Christie is the master class. Every suspect needs a motive, an opportunity, and a false reason to seem guilty. Plant the real clue early and bury it under three false ones of equal visual weight.
**Takeaways:**
  - Borrow the fair-play contract — show every clue the detective ultimately uses
  - Borrow honest red herrings and the closed-circle suspect pool
  - Watch for flattened character — Christie's suspects are sometimes types more than people; pair her clue-fairness with real interiority if the book wants deep characters

## READING
**Title:** The Big Sleep; The Long Goodbye; "The Simple Art of Murder" (essay)
**Author:** Raymond Chandler
**Status:** read
**Gives:** The hardboiled detective as a morally exhausted man moving through a corrupt world he can't fix, only survive. Voice-driven first-person narration where the similes do as much work as the plot.
**Tags:** genre-split, hardboiled, voice
**Correlation:** If the book wants moral weight — a detective changed or worn down by what they investigate, rather than one who solves and moves on unbothered — Chandler is the model. Worth reading directly against Doyle: Watson is warm and admiring, Marlowe is guarded and worn.
**Takeaways:**
  - Borrow the dry, metaphor-rich hardboiled first-person voice
  - Borrow the case-as-tour-of-a-broken-social-landscape approach
  - Watch for plot looseness — a deliberate stylistic choice for Chandler, not something to imitate by accident, especially if readers expect fair-play clues

## READING
**Title:** Gaudy Night; Strong Poison; the Lord Peter Wimsey series
**Author:** Dorothy L. Sayers
**Status:** read
**Gives:** A detective allowed to fall in love, doubt himself, and change over the course of a series — genuine long-arc character development inside a genre that mostly kept its detectives static.
**Tags:** genre-split, character-arc, series-structure
**Correlation:** The strongest classic-canon model for a detective with a real multi-book arc — want/need/wound, evolving relatability — without losing the puzzle-plot machinery.
**Takeaways:**
  - Borrow the technique for letting a detective actually grow across a series
  - Study how literary-ambition prose can sit comfortably inside genre plotting

## READING
**Title:** The Innocence of Father Brown
**Author:** G.K. Chesterton
**Status:** read
**Gives:** A detective whose method is psychological and moral insight rather than physical-clue deduction — Father Brown reasons from what kind of person could or couldn't do a given thing.
**Tags:** genre-split, non-forensic-detective, reasoning-method
**Correlation:** A genuinely different detective archetype from Holmes's "reasoning machine" — foundational and older than the modern profiler archetype, useful if the detective's gift is reading people rather than physical evidence.
**Takeaways:**
  - Borrow a reasoning-from-human-nature method as an alternative to physical-clue deduction

## READING
**Title:** The Name of the Rose
**Author:** Umberto Eco
**Status:** read
**Gives:** A murder mystery set in a medieval monastery, built explicitly as a tribute to Holmes — the detective-monk William of Baskerville is a direct homage.
**Tags:** adjacent, worldbuilding, homage
**Correlation:** Useful for seeing how a mystery structure can carry enormous worldbuilding and thematic weight without losing its puzzle spine — directly relevant when a detective story is also doing heavy worldbuilding.
**Takeaways:**
  - Study how the puzzle spine supports heavy thematic and world weight without collapsing

## READING
**Title:** When We Were Orphans
**Author:** Kazuo Ishiguro
**Status:** read
**Gives:** A literary novel that uses detective-story structure and imagery while subverting the genre's promise of a clean, satisfying solution.
**Tags:** adjacent, unreliable-narration, emotional-resolution
**Correlation:** Useful for studying unreliable narration and how a mystery's emotional resolution can matter more than its factual one.
**Takeaways:**
  - Study a mystery that resolves the character before it resolves the case

## READING
**Title:** In the Woods (Dublin Murder Squad series)
**Author:** Tana French
**Status:** read
**Gives:** Modern police-procedural craft with real literary prose — forensic and procedural realism balanced against deep character interiority.
**Tags:** adjacent, procedural, modern
**Correlation:** Updates the Sayers project — a detective with a real inner life — for a modern procedural context.
**Takeaways:**
  - Study the balance of forensic detail against a detective's inner life

## READING
**Title:** The Daughter of Time
**Author:** Josephine Tey
**Status:** read
**Gives:** A detective solves a five-hundred-year-old cold case from a hospital bed using only historical documents — pure deductive reasoning as narrative engine, almost no physical action.
**Tags:** adjacent, pure-deduction, low-action
**Correlation:** Useful for scenes where a detective reasons aloud with a companion and the reasoning itself is the drama — closer to Doyle's deduction set pieces than to modern thriller pacing.
**Takeaways:**
  - Study scenes where reasoning itself, not physical action, carries the tension
